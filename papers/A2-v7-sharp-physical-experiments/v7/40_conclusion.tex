\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:v7-conclusion}
The relative two-boundary law provides a statistical description of a rare physical record that remains valid as its length and the number of independent observations increase together. Its analytical content is the uniform comparison of nonlinear actions and relative determinant amplitudes, followed by integration against the actual residual-time measure. Positivity of the endpoint Hessian alone would not supply this comparison, because the normalizing twist is exponentially small.

The experiment transition makes the role of that uniformity explicit. Within the stated identical-even positive-offset regime, the endpoint coupling and the number of successful observations determine the discrepancy between the physical and boundary models. Counting raw preparations introduces a further rare-event factor. This is a quantitative statement about statistical approximation, not an assertion that long bridges reveal coordinates absent from short ones. The latter question is answered separately by the finite-jet and amplitude inverses.

For reconstruction, coarsening matters. The two endpoint variances provide a uniformly Lipschitz inverse for facing curvatures. Unlabelled count amplitudes pass through a repeated-root inverse and have a different accuracy scale. The calibrated Bernoulli procedure and its matched, confidence-dependent lower bound show how this distinction enters preparation cost in a specified physical design. The result neither replaces selected-position acquisition by count data nor removes the initial coarse bracket from the experiment.

All these statements concern the observations and uniformities specified in Table~\ref{tab:v7-experiments}. The general forward theory retains unequal curvatures, nonsymmetric smooth contacts, competing channel onsets, and the absence of a finite-horizon assumption. The circular formulas, marked responses, contact realization, and the source-pinned two-collision companion provide additional consequences without being used as substitutes for the relative proof. Together they separate what is identified, what is approximated, and what must be paid to observe it.
