\section{Intrinsic residual equations at every multiplication corank}
\label{sec:intrinsic-residual-germs}
The residual Fitting identities describe restrictions of intrinsic ideals.
To pass from these restrictions to a neighbourhood, one must retain the
nonlinear equations. We give such a presentation at every point. The
linear algebra in this section applies to a map of vector bundles on any
smooth complex variety; the polar formula identifies its intrinsic
obstruction for the multiplication systems of
Section~\ref{sec:polar-residual}.

The matrix normal derivative and the transverse determinantal model used
below are classical \cite[Remark 1.18; Section 1.3.2]{FKZ21}.
The point here is their explicit expression in multiplication polars,
including the joint relations between distinct annihilators, and the
nonlinear equations left when transversality fails.

\subsection{The joint polar obstruction}
Fix $U\in D$, and write
\[
 K_U=\ker\mu_U,\qquad C_U=\operatorname{coker}\mu_U,\qquad
 \rho=\dim C_U,\qquad s=\dim K_U=e-p+\rho.
\]
Here $C_U^*\subset F^*$ is the entire space of annihilators, not a
choice of one projective annihilator. Differentiating $\mu$ and then
restricting to its kernel and projecting to its cokernel defines
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intrinsic-rank-derivative}
 \partial_U:T_UX\longrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}(K_U,C_U),
 \qquad A\longmapsto[\dot\mu_U(A)|_{K_U}].
\end{equation}
This map is intrinsic. Indeed a change of source or target frame changes
$\dot\mu$ by terms of the form $P\mu_U+\mu_U Q$; these vanish after
restriction to $K_U$ and projection to $C_U$.

\begin{proposition}[Joint polar relations]\label{prop:joint-polar-relations}
For a symmetric multiplication system, the transpose of
\eqref{eq:intrinsic-rank-derivative} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:joint-polar-transpose}
 \begin{split}
 \partial_U^*:K_U\otimes C_U^*&\longrightarrow T_U^*X,\\
 \sum_i q_i\otimes\ell_i&\longmapsto
                    \sum_i B_{([\ell_i],U)}^*(q_i),
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
where the representative $\ell_i$ is used in the definition of $B$;
the expression is linear in that representative and extends to
$\ell_i=0$. Put
\[
 \mathcal R_U=\ker\partial_U^*,\qquad t=\rank\partial_U,
 \qquad b_U=\dim\mathcal R_U=\rho s-t.
\]
If $\rho=1$, a choice of nonzero annihilator identifies
$\mathcal R_U$ with $\ker\tau_{([\ell],U)}$. If $\rho>1$, the
kernel in \eqref{eq:joint-polar-transpose} includes relations between
distinct annihilators; it is not in general the direct sum of the
single-annihilator kernels.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $q\in K_U$, $\ell\in C_U^*$ and $A\in T_UX$, the natural
trace pairing gives
\[
 \langle q\otimes\ell,\partial_U(A)\rangle
        =\ell\dot\mu_U(A)(q)=B_{([\ell],U)}(A)(q).
\]
Summing proves the formula. If $C_U^*=\C\ell$, its kernel is
$K_U\cap\ker B^*=K_U\cap Q=\ker(\mu_U|_Q)$.
All identifications are compatible with changes of frame by the
intrinsic construction of $\partial_U$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{An exact residual presentation}
Let $H_U=\operatorname{Hom}(K_U,C_U)$ and
$H_0=\operatorname{im}\partial_U$. Choose a vector-space complement
$H_1$ so that $H_U=H_0\oplus H_1$, and choose an isomorphism
$L:\C^t\to H_0$. Such choices specify coordinates, not additional
hypotheses. In particular $H_1$ identifies with
$\operatorname{coker}\partial_U$ and has dimension $b_U$.

\begin{theorem}[Residual matrix germ]\label{thm:residual-matrix-germ}
At every $U\in D$ there are analytic coordinates
$(y_1,\ldots,y_t,x_1,\ldots,x_{M-t})$ centred at $U$ and an
$H_1$-valued convergent power series $\Psi(y,x)$ with
$\Psi(0)=d\Psi(0)=0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exact-residual-matrix-germ}
 \widehat{\mathcal O}_{D,U}\simeq
 \C[[y_1,\ldots,y_t,x_1,\ldots,x_{M-t}]]
                  /I_\rho\bigl(L(y)+\Psi(y,x)\bigr).
\end{equation}
The same presentation holds with convergent power series for the
analytic local ring. Moreover, for $1\le j\le\rho$, the ideal of
the locus where the original multiplication has rank at most
$p-\rho+j-1$ is, in this presentation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nested-residual-ranks}
 I_j\bigl(L(y)+\Psi(y,x)\bigr).
\end{equation}
These are equalities of ideals after analytic change of coordinates,
not merely tangent-space or set-theoretic assertions. No
expected-codimension assumption is required.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $r=p-\rho$. Trivialize source and target and choose an invertible
$r$-minor at $U$. After constant changes of frame the matrix is
\[
 \mu=\begin{pmatrix}A&B_0\\ C&D_0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 A(U)=I_r,\quad B_0(U)=C(U)=D_0(U)=0.
\]
Invertible analytic row and column operations reduce it to
$\operatorname{diag}(I_r,S)$, where
$S=D_0-CA^{-1}B_0$ is a $\rho\times s$ matrix vanishing at $U$.
If $r=0$, this means $S=\mu$, with no inverse block. The differential
of $S$ at $U$ is precisely \eqref{eq:intrinsic-rank-derivative};
the correction $CA^{-1}B_0$ starts in degree two.

Let $P_0:H_U\to H_0$ and $P_1:H_U\to H_1$ be the projections.
The map $L^{-1}P_0S$ has differential of rank $t$. Complete its
$t$ coordinate functions to analytic local coordinates $(y,x)$ on
$X$ using the inverse function theorem. Then $P_0S=L(y)$ exactly,
whereas $\Psi=P_1S$ has no constant or linear term. This proves the
claimed matrix expression. For a block diagonal matrix with an
invertible $r$-block,
\[
 I_{r+j}\bigl(\operatorname{diag}(I_r,S)\bigr)=I_j(S)
               \quad(1\le j\le\rho).
\]
To see both inclusions, a $j$-minor of $S$ extends by the identity
block to an $(r+j)$-minor; all other terms are minors of $S$ of size
at least $j$ and belong to $I_j(S)$. This proves
\eqref{eq:nested-residual-ranks} and, with $j=\rho$, the analytic
presentation of $D$. Completion gives
\eqref{eq:exact-residual-matrix-germ}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:residual-germ-invariance}
The power series $\Psi$ is not a canonical function: complements,
frames and coordinates enter its construction. The completed local
ring and the nested determinantal ideals are canonical. Their
compatibility on overlaps follows because each presentation is
obtained from the original ideals $I_{r+j}(\mu)$ by invertible
operations. The theorem therefore supplies local equations also at
support collisions and at points with several annihilators. It does
not replace $\Psi$ by its quadratic term or assert that a constant
polar-rank stratum has a product neighbourhood.
\end{remark}

The first nonzero residual terms can be computed without solving all
the equations. For example, along a curve with parameter $h$, write
\[
 \mu(h)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 A_0+hA_1+O(h^2)&hB_1+h^2B_2+O(h^3)\\
 hC_1+h^2C_2+O(h^3)&hD_1+h^2D_2+O(h^3)
 \end{pmatrix},\qquad A_0\text{ invertible}.
\]
Expansion of the inverse gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:second-schur-obstruction}
 S(h)=hD_1+h^2(D_2-C_1A_0^{-1}B_1)+O(h^3).
\end{equation}
For a velocity in $\ker\partial_U$, the class of the second
coefficient in $H_U/H_0$ is independent of the acceleration of the
curve. An acceleration changes that coefficient by an element of
$\operatorname{im}\partial_U$. The same argument, using the chain
rule and $D_1=0$, proves independence of coordinates and frames.
Thus \eqref{eq:second-schur-obstruction}, paired with
$\mathcal R_U=(H_U/H_0)^*$, is the intrinsic quadratic obstruction
on $\ker\partial_U$. It describes the quadratic part of
$\Psi(0,x)$; it is not by itself the initial ideal of all the
maximal minors.

\begin{corollary}[Transverse determinantal neighbourhoods]
\label{cor:transverse-determinantal-germ}
If $\mathcal R_U=0$, then $M\ge\rho s$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generic-determinantal-germ}
 \widehat{\mathcal O}_{D,U}\simeq
 \C[[z_1,\ldots,z_{M-\rho s},(y_{ij})_{1\le i\le\rho,\,1\le j\le s}]]
                         /I_\rho((y_{ij})).
\end{equation}
This is also an analytic product decomposition. The germ is reduced,
Cohen--Macaulay and of dimension $M-a$. If $\rho=1$ it is smooth;
if $\rho\ge2$ its singular support is the product of the smooth
factor with the rank-at-most-$(\rho-2)$ generic matrix locus.
This last assertion concerns reduced support, not equality of the
singular-scheme ideal with $I_{\rho-1}((y_{ij}))$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Here $t=\rho s$, $H_1=0$, and \eqref{eq:exact-residual-matrix-germ}
has no nonlinear residual part. Choosing the entries of $S$ as
coordinates proves \eqref{eq:generic-determinantal-germ}.
For completeness, the generic rank-at-most-$(\rho-1)$ matrix variety
is irreducible: it is the closure of products of a
$\rho\times(\rho-1)$ matrix and a $(\rho-1)\times s$ matrix.
Its rank-$(\rho-1)$ stratum has dimension
$(\rho-1)(s+1)$, as one sees by choosing its image and a surjection
onto it. Its codimension is $s-\rho+1=a$. The expected-grade
maximal-minor resolution \cite{EN} consequently gives a
Cohen--Macaulay quotient. At rank $\rho-1$, Schur elimination leaves
$a$ independent entries, so the quotient is generically smooth and
hence generically reduced. Cohen--Macaulayness gives $S_1$;
$R_0+S_1$ implies reducedness \cite{Stacks}.
At rank at most $\rho-2$, every first derivative of every maximal
minor vanishes. The tangent dimension is the ambient dimension,
strictly larger than the local dimension. These and only these
points are singular. For $\rho=1$ the ideal consists of independent
coordinates. The dimension and all product assertions follow.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Corank-one equations and their quadratic obstruction}
Corank one admits a smaller presentation than the general matrix
normal form: all independent linear equations may be eliminated.

\begin{theorem}[Minimal residual equations in expected codimension]
\label{thm:minimal-residual-equations}
Suppose $\mu_U$ has corank one, put $z=([\ell],U)$ and
$\delta=\dim\ker\tau_z$, and let $d=M-a$. There is an exact
presentation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:minimal-residual-local-ring}
 \widehat{\mathcal O}_{D,U}
       \simeq\C[[x_1,\ldots,x_{d+\delta}]]/(f_1,\ldots,f_\delta),
       \qquad f_i\in(x_1,\ldots,x_{d+\delta})^2,
\end{equation}
and an analogous convergent presentation. If $D$ has codimension
$a$ at $U$, these $\delta$ equations form a regular sequence. In
that case the displayed embedding dimension and number of equations
are minimal. Without that codimension hypothesis the exact
presentation still holds, but regularity of the sequence is not
asserted.

The quadratic obstruction takes values in $(\ker\tau_z)^*$.
For a tangent vector $(\lambda,A)\in T_z\mathcal I$, choose graph
lifts of the maps $A_\nu:U_\nu\to V/U_\nu$. On a monomial
$u_1\cdots u_m\in\Sym^mU_\nu$ define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quadratic-polar-obstruction}
 \begin{split}
 \mathcal H_{\lambda,A}(u_1\cdots u_m)
 ={}&\sum_{i<j}\ell\beta(u_1\cdots A_\nu u_i\cdots
                                A_\nu u_j\cdots u_m)\\
 &+\sum_i\lambda\beta(u_1\cdots A_\nu u_i\cdots u_m).
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Extend linearly to $\mathcal E_U$. Its restriction to
$\ker\tau_z$ is the intrinsic quadratic obstruction and equals the
degree-two part of the residual equations, after identifying their
coordinate space with $(\ker\tau_z)^*$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The incidence is the zero scheme of a rank-$e$ section on the smooth
$(M+p-1)$-dimensional variety $\Pj(F^*)\times X$.
Its differential is
\[
 T_{[\ell]}\Pj(F^*)\oplus T_UX\longrightarrow\mathcal E_U^*,
       \qquad (\lambda,A)\longmapsto\lambda\mu_U+B_z(A).
\]
The annihilator of its image is
$\ker\mu_U\cap\ker B_z^*=\ker\tau_z$; thus its rank is
$e-\delta$. Choose that many independent equations and eliminate
them, together with the corresponding coordinates, by the analytic
implicit function theorem. The resulting smooth space has dimension
$M+p-1-(e-\delta)=d+\delta$. Subtracting appropriate linear
combinations of the eliminated equations leaves $\delta$ equations
with zero differential. Projection from the incidence is a scheme
isomorphism here by Theorem~\ref{thm:polar-tangent}; this proves
\eqref{eq:minimal-residual-local-ring}.

If the local codimension is $a$, this local ring has dimension $d$.
In the regular local ring with $d+\delta$ parameters its ideal,
generated by $\delta$ elements, has height $\delta$. Such a
sequence is regular: a regular local ring is Cohen--Macaulay, and
a height-equal-to-length generating sequence is a partial system of
parameters, hence a regular sequence. Since all $f_i$ are quadratic
or higher, no further smooth coordinate can be eliminated, and a
regular sequence of this length minimally generates the ideal.

To compute the quadratic terms, trivialize the projective line near
$[\ell]$ and use the graph path
$(\ell+h\lambda,u_i+hA_\nu u_i)$. Expanding the incidence section,
its coefficient of $h^2$ is exactly
\eqref{eq:quadratic-polar-obstruction}. The order-$h$ coefficient
vanishes because $(\lambda,A)$ is tangent. A second-order change of
path changes the coefficient of $h^2$ by the image of the section's
differential, which is annihilated by $\ker\tau_z$. Changes of graph
lifts, projective trivialization or frames have precisely the same
effect, by the chain rule and the vanishing first-order section.
Thus the restriction is well defined. The nonlinear corrections used
in eliminating the independent equations are again second-order
changes of path and have zero pairing with this kernel. This proves
the asserted identification with the degree-two residual equations.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:higher-residual-terms}
Theorem~\ref{thm:minimal-residual-equations} identifies an actual
complete-intersection germ in expected codimension, not just its
tangent dimension. Its quadratic forms need not generate the full
initial ideal. Higher-order terms in the $f_i$ or in $\Psi$ must be
retained unless an additional normal-form argument removes them.
The simple-normal-determinant argument below supplies such an
argument on the residual wall. In the excess regime the same exact
equations remain available without silently imposing expected grade.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Realization in every symmetric degree}
The transverse class is not confined to two-point annihilators or
to quadratic binary multiplication. The following explicit model
realizes it for every symmetric degree and every possible
multiplication corank.

\begin{proposition}[Realization of transverse matrix germs]
\label{prop:every-degree-realization}
Let $m\ge2$, $e\ge p\ge1$ and $1\le\rho\le p$ be integers.
There exist a vector space $V$, a surjection
$\beta:\Sym^mV\to\C^p$, and $e$ one-dimensional contacts in
$X=\Gr(1,V)^e$ for which the multiplication has corank $\rho$
and $\mathcal R_U=0$. Consequently its failure germ is the full
generic $\rho\times(e-p+\rho)$ maximal-minor germ, times a
smooth factor, for every $m$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Put $r=p-\rho$ and take $V$ with basis
$x_1,\ldots,x_e,w_1,\ldots,w_\rho$. Let
$f_1,\ldots,f_p$ be the target basis. Define $\beta$ on monomials by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:every-degree-realization}
 \begin{aligned}
 \beta(x_i^m)&=f_i &&(1\le i\le r),\\
 \beta(x_j^{m-1}w_\alpha)&=\frac1m f_{r+\alpha}
                         &&(r<j\le e,\ 1\le\alpha\le\rho),
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
and set it equal to zero on all remaining basis monomials. The
listed monomials are distinct, including when $m=2$, so this defines
a linear map. It is surjective because $e-r=e-p+\rho\ge\rho>0$.
At $U_j=\C x_j$, the source of the contact multiplication has basis
$(x_j^m)_j$, its first $r$ columns are $f_1,\ldots,f_r$, and its
remaining columns are zero. Thus $K_U$ is spanned by the last
$s=e-r$ source vectors and $C_U$ by $f_{r+1},\ldots,f_p$.
Varying only the $j$th line from $x_j$ toward $w_\alpha$ has derivative
\[
 \left.\frac{d}{dh}\right|_{h=0}
       \beta((x_j+hw_\alpha)^m)=f_{r+\alpha},\qquad j>r.
\]
These $\rho s$ independent tangent directions map under
$\partial_U$ to every matrix unit in $\operatorname{Hom}(K_U,C_U)$.
The map is surjective, and Corollary~\ref{cor:transverse-determinantal-germ}
applies.
\end{proof}
This proposition concerns arbitrary symmetric linear systems. It
exhibits genuine higher-degree failure germs and supplies concrete
models for the intrinsic theorem. It is separate from, and does not
presuppose, a global classification for $\Sym^mU\to V_{mn}$ on the
binary Grassmannian. The latter system's two-point sectors remain
the subject of Section~\ref{sec:higher-products}.

\subsection{Two explicit nontransverse residual germs}
These familiar singularities provide direct tests of the distinction
between a residual space and the nonlinear residual equations.

\paragraph{A simultaneous-annihilator relation.}
For the bundle matrix on $\mathbb A^3$
\[
 \mu=\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\ y&z\end{pmatrix},
\]
the origin has $K=C=\C^2$. The intrinsic derivative is inclusion
of the symmetric matrices into all $2\times2$ matrices. Its transpose
has the one-dimensional kernel generated by
$e_1\otimes e_2^*-e_2\otimes e_1^*$. For each fixed nonzero
$\ell\in C^*$, however, the map
$K\to T_0^*\mathbb A^3$, $q\mapsto\partial^*(q\otimes\ell)$,
is injective: its coefficients are those of the product of two
nonzero linear binary forms. Thus checking one annihilator at a time
would miss the joint relation. In
Theorem~\ref{thm:residual-matrix-germ}, $H_0$ is the symmetric
subspace and $\Psi=0$; the exact failure germ is $xz-y^2=0$.
This example is a bundle-matrix example for the intrinsic theorem,
not an additional claim about binary subseries.

\paragraph{A cubic multiplication cusp.}\label{ex:cubic-residual-cusp}
Let $V=\C X\oplus\C Y\oplus\C Z$, $F=\C$, and define
$\beta:\Sym^3V\to F$ by
$\beta(Y^2Z)=1/3$, $\beta(X^3)=-1$, and zero on the other
basis monomials. Take one line contact, and use the chart
$U=\C(Z+xX+yY)$ near $\C Z$. Its multiplication matrix is the
one-entry matrix
\[
 \beta((Z+xX+yY)^3)=y^2-x^3.
\]
At the origin $\delta=1$ and the quadratic obstruction is $y^2$,
whereas the exact residual equation is $y^2-x^3$. The map from its
completed local ring to $\C[[t]]$ given by $x=t^2$, $y=t^3$
is injective: reducing a power series modulo $y^2-x^3$ leaves
$A(x)+yB(x)$, and the even powers in $A(t^2)$ cannot cancel the
odd powers in $t^3B(t^2)$. Thus the cusp has one branch, unlike the
double crossing. This explicit higher-product system shows why the
full residual equation, rather than only its quadratic part, occurs
in the theorem.

\begin{remark}[Formalism and classified germs]\label{rem:formalism-versus-classification}
Schur elimination and transverse determinantal normal forms are
classical. Here their multiplication-specific input is the joint
polar derivative and its residual relations. The primary formula
of Theorem~\ref{thm:quartic-primary} is a further assertion: its
weighted-minor spanning lemma determines the entire nonlinear ideal,
not only the existence of a residual matrix. Likewise
Theorem~\ref{thm:conductor-reduction} requires an actual surjectivity
identity before finite-algebra Fitting ideals can replace the
original multiplication ideals.
\end{remark}
