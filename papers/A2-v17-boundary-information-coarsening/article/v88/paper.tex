\begin{abstract}
We study matrix-valued probability curves obtained by normalizing a
simultaneously diagonalizable polynomial through two unknown stochastic
channels. A linear operator formed from the observations characterizes
recovery of the monic normalization. With full-rank channels, at
$2d+1$ clocks its nullity is exactly the degree of the common divisor of the component polynomials;
this clock count is sharp in the worst case, even with fixed positive
channels. Its smallest singular value and the product of the channel
singular values enter an additive, one-sided spectral modulus. The
modulus allows arbitrary competitors in the closed model and collisions
between components. In degree one we replace latent-factor optimization
by a canonical resolvent-residue invariant, identify its zero set, and
prove its semicontinuity and a stronger Lipschitz inverse. A direct
least-squares, singular-subspace and eigenvalue estimator attains this
inverse order. Honest confidence sets and matching minimax orders follow
on observable signal classes; two positive least-favourable families
remain least favourable under adaptive clock selection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Normalization removes a scalar amplitude from a matrix observation. It
can also remove a polynomial factor, and with it part of a latent
spectrum. The inverse problem therefore has two stages: recover a
normalization from projective data, and recover the spectrum of the
resulting matrix polynomial. Treating these stages as two unrelated
condition numbers can introduce a loss which the observation map does
not possess. The normalization error remains diagonal in the true
channel coordinates; it need not be amplified a second time by the
channel inverses.

We give a finite-clock formulation of this principle. The normalization
is characterized by an observable linear residual operator. For
polynomial degree $d$, its kernel has an exact algebraic description
once $2d+1$ clocks are available. For an arbitrary design, its rank is
the identification criterion, and its least singular value is the
normalization condition. This separates a worst-case clock count from
the information actually present in a particular curve.

The affine experiment admits a further reduction. Its normalized curve
has the form $A+B/(T-h)$. After recovering $h$, the residues of the
inverse reduced pencil give an intrinsic condition, including at
repeated eigenvalues. The condition is a finite observable construction,
not a maximum over latent factorizations. It has a specified zero set
and a sharp direction of semicontinuity. The spectral target remains
Lipschitz through partial action collisions, although the factors and
the residue partition may change. Two different boundaries---channel
rank loss and collapse of the entire projective curve---give sharp
statistical orders.

\subsection{The polynomial experiment}
Fix $d\ge1$, $2\le k\le\min(m_1,m_2)$, an interval $[L,D]$, distinct
clocks $D<T_1<\cdots<T_\ell$, with $\ell\ge d+1$, and
$0<\alpha_*<1/k$. The columns of
$U\in\R^{m_1\times k}$ and $V\in\R^{m_2\times k}$ are probability
vectors. Let $\alpha_b\ge\alpha_*$ and $\sum_b\alpha_b=1$. Put
\begin{align}
 f_b(z)&=\prod_{a=1}^d(z-\lambda_{ba}),\qquad \lambda_{ba}\in[L,D],
       &q(z)&=\sum_b\alpha_b f_b(z),\nonumber\\
 K(z)&=U\diag(\alpha_b f_b(z))V^{\mathsf T},
       &P(T)&=\frac{K(T)}{q(T)}.                  \label{eq:model}
\end{align}
The parameter set $\cK_d$ is compact; singular channels and repeated
roots are included. The polynomials $q$ and $f_b$ are monic of degree
$d$. All $q(T_j)$ and component probabilities
$p_b(T_j)=\alpha_b f_b(T_j)/q(T_j)$ have uniform positive bounds.
An arbitrary scalar detector multiplying $K(T)$ disappears on
normalization. Component-dependent detectors are not part of this
experiment.

The target $\Lambda$ is the unordered multiset of the $kd$ roots of all
$f_b$, counted with multiplicity. We do not assert recovery of a
partition of these roots into components. For two multisets of the same
cardinality write
\[
 d_\infty(\Lambda,\Lambda')=
       \min_\pi\max_a|\lambda_a-\lambda'_{\pi(a)}|,
 \qquad \delta=\max_j\|P(T_j)-P'(T_j)\|_F.
\]
For real multisets this is the maximum distance between their increasing
orders. All constants in this paper may depend on
$d,k,m_1,m_2,\alpha_*,L,D$ and the fixed clocks, but not on action gaps,
unless a gap is explicitly specified. There is no assertion uniform in
growing dimension or a vanishing weight floor.

Let $W=(T_j^r)_{j=1,\ldots,\ell;\,r=0,\ldots,d}$ and let $\Omega$
be the orthogonal projector onto $\operatorname{col}(W)^\perp$. Write
$q(z)=z^d+\sum_{r<d}a_rz^r$. For $c\in\R^d$ set
$r_c(z)=\sum_{r<d}c_rz^r$ and define
\begin{align}
 \cN_Pc&=(\Omega\otimes I)
       \big(r_c(T_j)\operatorname{vec}P(T_j)\big)_{j=1}^{\ell},
       &\tau(P)&=\sigma_{\min}(\cN_P),\nonumber\\
 b_P&=-(\Omega\otimes I)
       \big(T_j^d\operatorname{vec}P(T_j)\big)_{j=1}^{\ell},
       &\kappa&=\sigma_k(U)\sigma_k(V).           \label{eq:normalization}
\end{align}
Here $I$ has size $m_1m_2$, and the singular value uses Euclidean
coefficient norm and the product Frobenius norm. Thus $\tau$ is a
specified observable quantity, not a coordinate-free number without a
choice of norms. Changing an orthonormal basis of the residual space
does not change it. The normalization equation is $\cN_Pa=b_P$.

\begin{theorem}[Normalization, clock design, and spectral recovery]
\label{thm:main}
Suppose the true channels have full column rank.
If $\ell\ge2d+1$ and $g=\gcd(f_1,\ldots,f_k)$ is monic, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernel}
 \ker\cN_P=\{(q/g)s:\deg s<\deg g\},\qquad
 \dim\ker\cN_P=\deg g,
\end{equation}
where polynomials of degree less than $d$ are identified with their
coefficient vectors. In particular $\tau(P)>0$ exactly when the
component polynomials are relatively prime as a collection.

For any design with $\ell\ge d+1$ and $\tau(P)>0$, every competitor
in $\cK_d$ satisfies
\begin{align}
 \|a-a'\|_2&\le C\min\{1,\delta/\tau(P)\},        \label{eq:q-bound}\\
 d_\infty(\Lambda,\Lambda')&\le
 C\min\{1,[\delta(\kappa^{-1}+\tau(P)^{-1})]^{1/d}\}.
                                                        \label{eq:poly-bound}
\end{align}
If the roots within each true $f_b$ are simple and separated by at least
$\nu>0$, the exponent in \eqref{eq:poly-bound} is $1$, with a constant
also depending on $\nu$. Roots belonging to different components may
coincide. No rank or root-separation assumption is made on the competitor.

Conversely, at an interior parameter with simple roots within each
component, $\tau(P)=0$ gives a nonconstant local exact fibre with the
same channels and a changing root multiset. The count $2d+1$ is sharp
in the worst case for every fixed admissible $k$: there are relatively
prime, interior examples for which $2d$ clocks do not identify the
multiset. Richer curves can satisfy the rank criterion at fewer clocks.
\end{theorem}

In degree one write $f_b(z)=z-\xi_b$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:affine}
 h=\sum_b\alpha_b\xi_b,\quad
 A=U\diag(\alpha)V^{\mathsf T},\quad
 C=U\diag(\alpha_b\xi_b)V^{\mathsf T},\quad
 B=hA-C.
\end{equation}
Then $P(T)=A+B/(T-h)$ and $K(z)=zA-C$. At three clocks
$\tau(P)\asymp\gamma(P):=\|B\|_F$. Consequently
\eqref{eq:poly-bound} recovers the gap-free affine inverse with
$\sig=\kappa\gamma/(\kappa+\gamma)$, set to zero on either boundary.
Section~\ref{sec:residue} strengthens this to an observable condition
$\eta(P)$ constructed from inverse-pencil residues. It satisfies
$\eta(P)\ge c\sig_*(P)$, where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:envelope}
 \sig_*(P)=\max\{\sig(\omega):\mathcal P(\omega)=P\}.
\end{equation}
Theorem~\ref{thm:residue} gives a one-sided Lipschitz bound with
$\eta$ in place of $\sig_*$. Theorem~\ref{thm:algorithm} gives a
direct spectral estimator, and Theorem~\ref{thm:statistics} proves
matching minimax orders on both signal classes. These are worst-case
class orders, with pointwise lower bounds on two explicit families;
they are not a claim that every anisotropic singular point has the
same local minimax geometry.

\subsection{Relation to previous work}
The equation $\cN_Pa=b_P$ is a vector rational-interpolation condition.
Rational interpolation and its solution spaces have a substantial
history; see Van Barel--Bultheel \cite{VBB}. We do not claim that
linearizing rational interpolation is new. The content here is the
identification of its defect with the common latent polynomial factor,
its realization by positive fixed-channel fibres, and its combination
with a one-sided spectral modulus in observation norm.

Nor is multiset perturbation of a regular pencil new. Bauer--Fike
\cite{BF} provides the basic exclusion argument. Chu's generalized
pencil theorem \cite{Chu87} already treats eigenvalue clusters and
relates conditioning to regularity and eigenvector deficiency; his
matrix-polynomial extension \cite{Chu03} includes multiple eigenvalues
of monic and regular polynomials. Our root-counting lemma is an
explicit specialization of this resolvent-and-homotopy mechanism, not
a claim to have discovered perturbation through repeated eigenvalues.
The additional step is recovering an unknown scalar polynomial from
normalized observations while retaining the diagonal form of its error.

Higham--Mackey--Tisseur \cite{HMT} compare the conditioning of a matrix
polynomial with that of its linearizations. In particular their
simple-eigenvalue formula involves the derivative of the polynomial;
it is not a uniform estimate through all component-root collisions.
Tisseur--Higham \cite{TH} develop structured polynomial pseudospectra,
and Su--Bai \cite{SB} study linearization of rational eigenvalue
problems. Those results start with a specified polynomial or rational
matrix function and a coefficient perturbation model. The present
normalization rank, positive exact fibres, and additive conversion
from categorical observation error are separate assertions. Our
residue invariant uses the classical resolvent; the new bound also
uses the exact projective pole-shift fibre before applying it.

Observable latent diagonalization and factor identification are
classical \cite{AMR,BJR}. Robust tensor uniqueness, including
quantitative Kruskal-type results, is treated by
Bhaskara--Charikar--Vijayaraghavan \cite{BCV}. Here the target is a
multiset, not a stable naming of individual rank-one factors; repeated
actions can destroy the latter without destroying the former.
Li--Lee--Bresler \cite{LLB} give a general nuisance-transformation
perspective on bilinear identification. The passage from inverse
moduli to inference is classical \cite{Donoho,CaiLow,Dufour}, as is
the occurrence of singular rates in finite mixtures \cite{HN}.
The target-dependent distinction between partial spectral collisions
and complete projective collapse is established below for the stated
controlled categorical experiment.
