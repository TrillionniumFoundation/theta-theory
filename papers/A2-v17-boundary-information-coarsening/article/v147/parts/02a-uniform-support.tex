\section{A uniform contraction theorem and its inverse consequence}
\label{sec:uniform-support}

The support mechanism is not confined to quartics.  In this section let
\(\dim V=n\ge2\), \(W=\Sym^2V\), \(N=\dim W=n(n+1)/2\), and
\(D=\det V\).  Polarization defines a natural injection
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniform-j}
 j_n:D\otimes\Sym^nV\longrightarrow\bigwedge^nW,\qquad
 j_n(\epsilon\otimes\ell^n)=
       (\ell x_1)\wedge\cdots\wedge(\ell x_n),
 \quad \epsilon=x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_n.
\end{equation}
Changing the basis multiplies both sides by the same determinant.
The map is nonzero and its source is irreducible, so it is injective.
For \(q\in W^*=\Sym^2V^*\) put \(\kappa_{n,q}=\iota_q\circ j_n\).
We regard \(q\) as the symmetric map \(V\to V^*\); when it is
invertible, \(q^{-1}:V^*\to V\) defines an element of \(\Sym^2V\).
The pairing convention is \(\langle q,xy\rangle=q(x,y)\).

\begin{theorem}[The contraction kernel in every dimension]
\label{thm:uniform-contraction}
For every nonzero \(q\in\Sym^2V^*\), the following equalities hold:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniform-kernels}
 \ker\kappa_{n,q}=
 \begin{cases}
 D\otimes\Sym^n(\rad q),&q\text{ singular},\\
 0,&q\text{ nondegenerate and }n\text{ odd},\\
 D\otimes\C(q^{-1})^{n/2},&q\text{ nondegenerate and }n\text{ even}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Thus for \(\rank q=r<n\) the kernel dimension is
\(\binom{2n-r-1}{n}\).  For full rank it is zero or one according
to the parity of \(n\).
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Shears and the singular kernel}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform-contraction} for singular \(q\)]
Choose \(V=A\oplus B\), where \(B=\rad q\) and \(q|_A\)
is nondegenerate.  Pure powers from \(B\) are killed by contraction,
so \(D\otimes\Sym^nB\subset\ker\kappa_{n,q}\).
The stabilizer of \(q\) contains the \(B\)-scaling torus, all
shears \(a+b\mapsto a+b+L(a)\), and \(O(A,q|_A)\times GL(B)\).
Both the shears and the torus preserve \(q\): adding a radical
vector to either argument does not change its value.  On
\(D\otimes\Sym^nV\) the torus has the ordinary polynomial
weights shifted by the constant \(\dim B\), its determinant
weight; no weight spaces are identified by that shift.
Consequently any additional kernel vector would, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:shear-descent}, give a nonzero kernel vector in
\(D\otimes A\otimes\Sym^{n-1}B\).
This module is irreducible for the last product group.  Here the
standard module of the full \(O_2\), not just \(SO_2\), is used:
a reflection exchanges its two weight lines.  The rank-one case is
one-dimensional; in higher rank the standard orthogonal module is
irreducible.  The determinant character only twists this assertion.

Choose \(a=x_1\in A\) with \(q(a,a)=1\), a nonzero \(b\in B\),
and complete to a basis with \(q(a,x_i)=0\) for \(i>1\).
Differentiate \eqref{eq:uniform-j} at \(\ell=b+ta\) and contract.
Only its first factor contributes in degree one in \(t\), giving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniform-singular-nonzero}
 n\kappa_{n,q}(\epsilon\otimes ab^{n-1})
       =(bx_2)\wedge\cdots\wedge(bx_n)\ne0.
\end{equation}
Multiplication by \(b\) is injective on \(V\), which proves the
nonvanishing.  The restriction to the irreducible degree-one module
is therefore injective, a contradiction.  This proves the singular
case, including all radical dimensions.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A cofactor identity for the scalar kernel}

The exceptional even-dimensional line admits a proof that does not
require a decomposition of the exterior-power target.  The cofactor
divergence cancellation is the exterior-valued version of the classical
Piola identity, rather than a new divergence identity; see
\cite[equation (1.1), \S3.1]{KupfermanShachar}.  Its use below combines
that cancellation with the algebraic Gaussian moment recurrence.

\begin{lemma}[Algebraic cofactor cancellation]
\label{lem:wick-cofactor}
For \(q(x_i,x_j)=\delta_{ij}\) and \(\rho=\sum_i x_i^2\),
\(\kappa_{n,q}(\epsilon\otimes\rho^{n/2})=0\) when \(n\) is even.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Introduce indeterminates \(t_1,\ldots,t_n\), write
\(\ell(t)=\sum_i t_i x_i\), and set
\[
 a_i(t)=\ell(t)x_i,\qquad
 A_i(t)=(-1)^{i-1}a_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat a_i
                         \wedge\cdots\wedge a_n.
\]
The symmetry \(\partial_{t_i}a_j=x_ix_j=\partial_{t_j}a_i\)
implies the polynomial identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cofactor-divergence}
 \sum_i\partial_{t_i}A_i=0.
\end{equation}
Indeed the term differentiating \(a_j\) in \(A_i\) cancels
with the term differentiating \(a_i\) in \(A_j\): for \(i<j\),
moving their common factor to the same position changes the sign
by \((-1)^{j-i-1}\), opposite to their cofactor signs.

Define a linear functional \(G:\C[t_1,\ldots,t_n]\to\C\) by
\(G(t^\alpha)=0\) if some \(\alpha_i\) is odd, and otherwise
\(G(t^\alpha)=\prod_i(\alpha_i-1)!!\), with \((-1)!!=1\).
The monomial recurrence gives
\(G(t_iF)=G(\partial_{t_i}F)\); this is an algebraic identity,
not an appeal to convergence of a Gaussian integral.  If \(n=2r\),
the multinomial formula gives
\(G(\ell(t)^n)=(n-1)!!\rho^r\).
On the other hand contraction of \eqref{eq:uniform-j} gives
\[
 \kappa_{n,q}(\epsilon\otimes\ell(t)^n)=\sum_i t_i A_i(t).
\]
Apply \(G\) coefficientwise and use \eqref{eq:cofactor-divergence}.
The result is \((n-1)!!\kappa_{n,q}(\epsilon\otimes\rho^r)=0\),
as required in characteristic zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform-contraction} for nondegenerate \(q\)]
Work in an orthonormal basis, and put
\(\mathcal H_m=\ker(\sum_i\partial_{x_i}^2:\Sym^mV\to\Sym^{m-2}V)\).
The classical harmonic decomposition is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniform-harmonics}
 D\otimes\Sym^nV=
   \bigoplus_{t=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}D\otimes\rho^t\mathcal H_{n-2t}.
\end{equation}
The summands are irreducible and pairwise inequivalent for the full
orthogonal group by Lemma~\ref{lem:full-orthogonal-harmonics}.  One can obtain the direct sum
by applying the Laplacian to powers of \(\rho\); the nonzero
coefficient on \(\rho^th_m\) is
\(2t(2m+2t+n-2)\).  The harmonic summand has highest weight
\((m,0,\ldots,0)\) for \(n\ge3\).  For \(n=2\) and \(m>0\)
its two \(SO_2\)-weight lines are exchanged by the other component
of \(O_2\), so it is again irreducible.  For \(m=0\) it is the
scalar module.  These facts also specify the determinant twists in
\eqref{eq:uniform-harmonics}.

It suffices to show that every summand with \(m=n-2t>0\) has
nonzero image.  Put
\[
 h_m=\tfrac12\bigl((x_1+\sqrt{-1}x_2)^m+
                         (x_1-\sqrt{-1}x_2)^m\bigr),\qquad
 \tau=(x_1x_2)\wedge\cdots\wedge(x_1x_n).
\]
Then \(h_m\in\mathcal H_m\).  Polarization in
\eqref{eq:uniform-j} gives, for a degree-\(n\) polynomial \(f\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniform-coefficient-rule}
 [\tau]\kappa_{n,q}(\epsilon\otimes f)=
 [x_1^n]f-\binom n2^{-1}\sum_{i=2}^n[x_1^{n-2}x_i^2]f.
\end{equation}
To check the coefficient, a contracted factor must be a square.
Contracting the first column leaves \(\tau\) only for \(x_1^n\).
Contracting column \(i>1\) leaves it only when that column is
\(x_i^2\) and the first is \(x_1x_i\); its wedge sign is minus.
The source monomial has multinomial coefficient \(\binom n2\).
No other placement can leave all the factors of \(\tau\).

For \(f=\rho^t h_m\), the coefficient of \(x_1^n\) is one
and the sum in \eqref{eq:uniform-coefficient-rule} is
\((n-1)t-\binom m2\).  Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniform-harmonic-nonzero}
 [\tau]\kappa_{n,q}(\epsilon\otimes\rho^t h_m)
   =\frac{m(n+m-2)}{n(n-1)}\ne0\qquad(m>0).
\end{equation}
Equivariance and irreducibility imply injectivity on each such
summand.  Pairwise inequivalence and complete reducibility prevent
cancellation between them.  For odd \(n\) this exhausts the source.
For even \(n\), the remaining line is killed by
Lemma~\ref{lem:wick-cofactor}.  A change of basis treats every
nondegenerate \(q\), and proves the theorem.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exact support and reconstruction in all dimensions at least four}

For \(0\ne z\in\bigwedge^nW\) its exterior support is the smallest
subspace \(S\subset W\) with \(z\in\bigwedge^n S\).  Its dimension
is the rank of the first skew-flattening
\(c_z:W^*\to\bigwedge^{n-1}W\), \(q\mapsto\iota_qz\).
Indeed coordinates along \(\ker c_z\) show that
\(S=(\ker c_z)^\perp\).  This is the classical enclosing-space
construction, not a new notion of rank
\cite[\S10.1]{LandsbergOttaviani}\cite[\S2.4]{Sheridan}.
The essential-variable space of a form \(f\), in contrast, is a
subspace of \(V\), the annihilator of
\(\{\eta\in V^*: \partial_\eta f=0\}\) \cite{Carlini}.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform support table]
\label{thm:uniform-support-table}
Let \(0\ne f\in\Sym^nV\) have \(d\) essential variables.
For \(0\ne\epsilon\in D\), the exterior support of
\(j_n(\epsilon\otimes f)\) has dimension
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniform-support-table}
 s_n(f)=
 \begin{cases}
 N-\binom{n-d+1}{2},&d<n,\\
 N-1,&d=n,\ n\text{ even, and }f\in\C^*h^{n/2}
                         \text{ for a nondegenerate quadratic }h,\\
 N,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
For every \(n\ge4\), every form with at least three essential
variables satisfies \(s_n(f)>2n\).  Consequently its image is
outside the closed second secant variety of \(\Gr(n,\Sym^2V)\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If the essential-variable space is \(H\ne V\),
Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform-contraction} says that the annihilating
forms \(q\) are exactly \(\Sym^2(V/H)^*\).  A nondegenerate
annihilator would make \(f\) a power of a nondegenerate quadratic,
which has essential-variable space \(V\): differentiating that
power and cancelling a nonzero polynomial proves the assertion.
The first support formula follows by taking the codimension of
\(\Sym^2(V/H)^*\) in \(W^*\).

If \(H=V\), there is no nonzero singular annihilator.  The
nondegenerate case of the same theorem leaves precisely the stated
even-dimensional exception.  The projective quadratic root of
\(h^{n/2}\) is unique, by factorization in a polynomial ring.
Thus all nonzero annihilators in the exceptional case are proportional,
and the annihilator space has dimension one.

For \(3\le d<n\), the first formula is at least \(3n-3>2n\)
when \(n\ge4\).  For \(d=n\), even its lower bound \(N-1\)
is greater than \(2n\).  A sum of two decomposable \(n\)-vectors
has support at most \(2n\).  The locus defined by this support bound
is closed, being a minors locus, so it contains the closed second
secant variety.  This last containment is classical
\cite[\S6.1]{LandsbergOttaviani2010}\cite[\S10.1]{LandsbergOttaviani}.
\end{proof}

The determinant of the gradients defines a natural Jacobian map
\[
 C_n:\bigwedge^n\Sym^2V\longrightarrow D\otimes\Sym^nV.
\]
For \(q_1\wedge\cdots\wedge q_n\), in the basis used above, it is
\(\epsilon\otimes\det(\partial q_i/\partial x_j)\).
Evaluating on \((x_1^2)\wedge(x_1x_2)\wedge\cdots\wedge(x_1x_n)\)
and then using pure powers proves \(C_nj_n=2\,\mathrm{id}\).
Hence \(Q_n=\frac12j_nC_n\) and \(P_n=1-Q_n\) are canonical
complementary projections.  There is no assumption that the
complement \(\ker C_n\) is irreducible.

\begin{theorem}[A family of inverse problems beyond dimension four]
\label{thm:uniform-recombination}
Let \(n\ge4\).  On \(X=\Gr(n,\Sym^2V)\), let \(U_n\) be the
open locus where the Jacobian has at least three essential variables.
This locus is nonempty.  Both \(P_nw\) and \(Q_nw\) are nonzero
there, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniform-pencil-section}
 \Pj(\C P_nw\oplus\C Q_nw)\cap X=\{[w]\}
\end{equation}
as schemes.  The analogous relative intersection over \(U_n\)
is the tautological section and remains so after every base change,
including nonreduced base change.  In particular the pair
\(([P_nw],[Q_nw])\) determines \([w]\), and an element of
\(\PGL(V)\) transporting both members of the pair transports the
underlying relation space.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The first-catalecticant rank condition shows that \(U_n\) is open.
For \(R=\langle x_1^2,\ldots,x_n^2\rangle\), the Jacobian is
\(2^n x_1\cdots x_n\), with \(n\) essential variables, so this
open is nonempty in every dimension under consideration.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform-support-table}, \(Q_nw\) has support
greater than \(2n\), whereas \(w\) has support \(n\).
Neither projection can therefore vanish.

Every quadratic Pluecker relation restricts on the component line as
\(F(aP_nw+bQ_nw)=(b-a)\ell_F(a,b)\).  If the linear forms
\(\ell_F\) span a two-dimensional space, their ideal sheaf on
\(\Pj^1\) is the unit ideal, and the intersection is the reduced
point \([1:1]\).  If their span has dimension one, the intersection
is a degree-two divisor: either it contains a second decomposable
point or it is doubled at \([w]\).  A second point would express
\(Q_nw\) as a linear combination of two decomposable vectors.
A double point would put \(Q_nw\) in
\(\widehat T_wX=\bigwedge^{n-1}R\wedge W\).
Every vector in that affine tangent space is a sum of at most \(n\)
terms, one for each omitted basis vector of \(R\); it is supported
on \(R\) and at most \(n\) further directions.  Both cases thus
contradict support greater than \(2n\).  If all \(\ell_F\) vanish,
the entire line is contained in \(X\), giving the same tangent
contradiction.  This proves \eqref{eq:uniform-pencil-section}.

For the relative assertion, the tautological Pluecker line and its
two projections identify the component-line bundle with
\(\Pj^1\times U_n\), with tautological section \([1:1]\).
In local frames the coefficients of the \(\ell_F\) form a
matrix with two columns.  Its rank is two on every geometric fibre,
so its two-by-two minors generate the unit ideal on \(U_n\).
Locally two rows are invertible; hence the restricted ideal sheaf
is exactly \((b-a)\).  That calculation is preserved under every
base change, with no reducedness assumption on the new base.
The final equivariance statement follows from the naturality of
\(j_n\) and \(C_n\).
\end{proof}


\begin{example}[A nondiagonal family in every dimension]
\label{ex:nondiagonal-uniform-web}
For \(n\ge4\) and \(t\ne0\), put
\[
 R_t=\langle x_1^2+t x_2x_3,x_2^2,\ldots,x_n^2\rangle.
\]
Its Jacobian is \(2^n x_1\cdots x_n\), by expansion along the
first column of the gradient matrix.  The \(n\) first partials
have distinct monomial supports, so the essential-variable dimension
is \(n\).  The product is squarefree, hence not a positive power
of a quadratic.  Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform-support-table} gives full
exterior support \(n(n+1)/2>2n\), and
Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform-recombination} applies.

This web is not a projective change of the diagonal web.  A quadric
\(a_1(x_1^2+t x_2x_3)+\sum_{i>1}a_ix_i^2\) with \(a_1\ne0\)
has rank at least two: its isolated nonzero \(x_1^2\) block and its
nonzero \(x_2x_3\) block contribute separately.  For \(a_1=0\)
it has rank one exactly at the \(n-1\) square points
\([x_2^2],\ldots,[x_n^2]\).  The diagonal web has \(n\) such
points, an invariant distinction.  This example belongs to the uniform
component-reconstruction open, not to the smooth-Jacobian locus:
its Jacobian is a normal-crossing hypersurface.  For \(n=5,t=1\)
it gives an explicit nondiagonal example with exterior support fifteen.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[What is, and is not, dimension-specific]
\label{rem:uniform-versus-intrinsic}
The kernel, support, and component-pair inverse theorems hold in all
specified dimensions.  For \(n=4\), the intrinsic normal cone of
the multiplication-failure scheme supplies precisely that pair,
which is the additional geometric input proved below.  We do not
assert that the same failure-scheme readout has been proved for
\(n>4\).  Thus the uniform result is an actual nonempty family of
inverse problems, not a claim that every step of the four-dimensional
geometric theorem automatically generalizes.  The classical
skew-flattening containment is used only after the restricted kernel
has been calculated.  A separate intrinsic extension, for a different
family of actual Fitting failure schemes in every dimension, is proved
in Section~\ref{sec:structural-coefficient-principle}.
\end{remark}
