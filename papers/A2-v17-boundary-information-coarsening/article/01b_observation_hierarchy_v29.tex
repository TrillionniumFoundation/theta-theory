\subsection{Observation hierarchy and the information retained by each coarsening}
\label{subsec:v23-observation-hierarchy}

The same physical preparation generates several statistically distinct
experiments.  To compare their sigma-fields, fix a parameter-independent
policy \(\pi\) with a deterministic preparation cap \(N\), as in
Section~\ref{sec:v16-adaptive}, and start with its complete stopped physical
history.  This history retains every chosen design, all collision points,
labels and flight times within every preparation, the success/failure
sequence, and the stopping time \(T\le N\).  Independent policy randomness
may also be recorded when it belongs to the specified experiment.  Full
collision histories are encoded in the common physical coordinates as
finite marked arrays.  Their countable disjoint union, followed by the
finite products and disjoint unions imposed by \(T\le N\), gives one
standard Borel stopped-history space.  Geometry changes its law, not its
underlying measurable space.  No total-variation replacement theorem for
this full growing collision array is asserted here.

The per-preparation endpoint--time map is a building block, not a separate
stopped experiment.  On the prescribed bridge event it retains the first
and last signed endpoints and the residual preparation-to-first-impact
time; on failure it retains a failure atom.  This is the one-record map for
which Theorem~\ref{thm:v6-transfer} proves the finite-to-boundary comparison.
Applying it separately to the preparations in the stopped history defines
the following hierarchy on a single sample space.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Full stopped collision history.}  The complete history just
described retains all within-preparation collision data, designs, failures
and the stopping time.

\item \emph{Stopped endpoint--time acquisition transcript.}  Replace each
within-preparation history by its endpoint--time record, retaining every
design, every failure, and \(T\).  Recorded policy randomness is retained
consistently.  Theorem~\ref{thm:v16-adaptive-transfer} compares the laws of
this transcript, with every failed preparation charged.

\item \emph{Count--endpoint transcript.}  Delete residual times from item 2,
but retain the complete design list, the success/failure sequence, the
successful endpoint pairs, and the stopping time.  The failure sequence may
equivalently be encoded by waiting counts when the final censored failure
run and the stopping information are also retained.

\item \emph{Endpoint-output experiment.}  Delete waiting counts and residual
times, retaining the successful endpoint pairs required by the specified
output rule and its cap cemetery symbol.  Any output labels are precisely
those specified by that rule, rather than an unannounced extra transcript.
\end{enumerate}

Let \(H^\pi\), \(T_{\rm ET}^\pi\), \(T_{\rm CE}^\pi\), and
\(T_{\rm out}^\pi\) denote these four records.  Their deterministic
forgetting maps give, after pullback to the full stopped-history space,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:v29-stopped-hierarchy}
 \sigma(T_{\rm out}^\pi)\subseteq\sigma(T_{\rm CE}^\pi)
       \subseteq\sigma(T_{\rm ET}^\pi)\subseteq\sigma(H^\pi).
\end{equation}
Common randomized output maps are Markov coarsenings as well; their
independent seeds can be adjoined when a sigma-field formulation is needed.
The coarsened record is always the output of the same acquisition policy.
It is not the history of a different policy obtained by retrospectively
withholding observations from the design rule.  In particular, retained
adaptive designs can encode part of a deleted record.  The asymptotic
identifications below are those of the protocols and hypotheses in the cited
theorems, not a claim that deleting times from every adaptive policy produces
the same limiting experiment.

These common Markov coarsenings produce genuinely different asymptotic
experiments.  At the fastest local geometric scale, the endpoint--time
protocol of Theorem~\ref{thm:v23-complete-et} converges to its Poisson
boundary-shift experiment; the positive density at the moving residual-time
ceiling yields shape resolution of order \(k^{-1}\).  At that scale the
waiting counts are asymptotically ancillary under \(j=o(\sqrt k)\), with the
other hypotheses of that theorem in force.

After residual time is removed, the ceiling density is integrated into a
linearly vanishing endpoint density.  The count channel then matters.  In the
multirate coordinate of Theorem~\ref{thm:v23-count-endpoint-joint}, the waiting
counts yield a Gaussian shift in the hyperbolic-exponent direction at shape
scale \((j\sqrt k)^{-1}\), while the iso-hyperbolic endpoint directions are
visible at the slower boundary scale
\(k\delta^2\log(1/\delta)\asymp1\).  Removing the waiting counts in that
protocol produces the endpoint-output experiment and its endpoint Gaussian
limit.

This hierarchy is consistent with the earlier fixed-table comparison.  If
\(q_j=e^{-j\gamma}\), the endpoint--residual record and the endpoint-only
record have different critical scales: the former reacts at order \(kq_j\),
whereas the latter reacts at order
\(kq_j^2\log(1/q_j)\).  Thus a weak one-record limit does not determine the
information scale, and integrating out residual time is not an innocuous
presentation choice.

The distinction between statistical information and physical cost is also
maintained.  For a selected bridge, success has probability of order
\(d^2e^{-j\gamma}\), so obtaining \(k\) successful records requires an
exponentially larger number of preparations.  Failed preparations remain in
the stopped transcript whenever that transcript is the experiment under
study.  Conditioning on success is used only when a theorem explicitly
studies the successful mark law.

Finally, the record hierarchy should not be confused with geometric
registration.  The local statistical realization in
Section~\ref{sec:v22-raw-physical} uses one fixed laboratory chart so that all
nearby parameters live on a common observation space.  The global rigidity
theorem of Section~\ref{sec:v23-intrinsic-multichannel-rigidity}, by contrast,
does not supply relative Euclidean placements between distinct measured
channels; those placements are reconstructed through analytic gluing.

A common choice of orientation is a third, separate issue.  The unoriented
classification uses the orbit of the entire marked signed law collection
under one simultaneous reversal, as specified in
Definition~\ref{def:v28-common-orientation-datum}.  The reversal transports the
lattice orientation convention and all channel signs together; it does not
alter deck labels or forget the relative sign choices.  This law-valued
quotient is not another item in the above hierarchy of Markov coarsenings.
In particular, replacing each endpoint pair by its absolute values, averaging
a law with its reflection, or independently discarding the orientation of
each channel gives different data.  The signed inverse is not invoked as an
inverse of any of those coarsenings.  The precise common-orientation
classification and its proof are given in
Theorem~\ref{thm:v28-unoriented-classification}.
