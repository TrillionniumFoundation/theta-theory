\subsection{Observation hierarchy and the information retained by each coarsening}
\label{subsec:v23-observation-hierarchy}

The same physical preparation generates several statistically distinct
experiments.  We use the following nested sigma-fields throughout the paper.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Full collision history.}  All collision points, labels, flight
times and the number of collisions during the preparation are retained.  No
total-variation replacement theorem for this growing array is asserted here.

\item \emph{Per-preparation endpoint--time record.}  On the prescribed bridge
event we retain the first and last signed endpoints and the residual
preparation-to-first-impact time; on failure we retain a failure atom.  This is
the record for which Theorem~\ref{thm:v6-transfer} proves the finite-to-boundary
comparison.

\item \emph{Stopped endpoint--time acquisition transcript.}  A policy retains
every design, every record from item 2, the success/failure sequence and the
stopping time.  The adaptive comparison of
Theorem~\ref{thm:v16-adaptive-transfer} applies to this complete stopped
transcript, with every failed preparation charged.

\item \emph{Count--endpoint transcript.}  Residual times are removed from item
3, but the designs, success/failure sequence or equivalently the waiting
counts, the successful endpoint pairs and the stopping time are retained.

\item \emph{Endpoint-output experiment.}  Waiting counts and residual times are
removed, retaining the successful endpoint pairs required for inference and
the cap cemetery symbol.
\end{enumerate}

These common Markov coarsenings produce genuinely different asymptotic
experiments.  At the fastest local geometric scale, item 3 converges to the
Poisson boundary-shift experiment of
Theorem~\ref{thm:v23-complete-et}; its positive density at the moving residual-
time ceiling yields shape resolution of order \(k^{-1}\).  At that scale the
waiting counts are asymptotically ancillary under \(j=o(\sqrt k)\).

After residual time is removed, the ceiling density is integrated into a
linearly vanishing endpoint density.  The count channel then matters.  In the
multirate coordinate of Theorem~\ref{thm:v23-count-endpoint-joint}, the waiting
counts yield a Gaussian shift in the hyperbolic-exponent direction at shape
scale \((j\sqrt k)^{-1}\), while the iso-hyperbolic endpoint directions are
visible at the slower boundary scale
\(k\delta^2\log(1/\delta)\asymp1\).  Removing the waiting counts produces item
5 and leaves precisely the v22 endpoint-output Gaussian experiment.

This hierarchy is consistent with the earlier fixed-table comparison.  If
\(q_j=e^{-j\gamma}\), the endpoint--residual record and the endpoint-only
record have different critical scales: the former reacts at order \(kq_j\),
whereas the latter reacts at order
\(kq_j^2\log(1/q_j)\).  Thus a weak one-record limit does not determine the
information scale, and integrating out residual time is not an innocuous
presentation choice.

The distinction between statistical information and physical cost is also
maintained.  For a selected bridge, success has probability of order
\(d^2e^{-j\gamma}\), so obtaining \(k\) successful records requires an
exponentially larger number of preparations.  Failed preparations remain in
the stopped transcript whenever that transcript is the experiment under
study.  Conditioning on success is used only when a theorem explicitly
studies the successful mark law.

Finally, the record hierarchy should not be confused with geometric
registration.  The local statistical realization in
Section~\ref{sec:v22-raw-physical} uses one fixed laboratory chart so that all
nearby parameters live on a common observation space.  The global rigidity
theorem of Section~\ref{sec:v23-intrinsic-multichannel-rigidity}, by contrast,
does not supply relative Euclidean placements between distinct measured
channels; those placements are reconstructed through analytic gluing.
