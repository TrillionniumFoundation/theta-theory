\begin{abstract}
We study metric quotients of singular polynomial observations through ramification, one-sided profiling, and native information. A residual-scale reduction replaces a tube-wide derivative gap by a weaker condition at the scale of the minimizing residual. Proper real corner presentations give exact divisorial tests for these derivative certificates and distinguish their boundaries; a critical example has an explicitly determined nonlinear residual. For endpoint profiling, we reconstruct the full minimal fibre after one visible cell disappears in every endpoint dimension at least three. In dimension two we classify all three-cell fibres by a scalar modulus, and distinguish cell loss from Hessian coalescence and rank loss. For calibrated binary polynomial observations with arbitrary separated double clusters and up to two simple endpoints, interpolation forces the inverse efficient information into a finite Hankel moment cone of dimension twice the retained dimension minus one. We determine its secants, the exact dual-query complexity, and the corresponding information secants. Total positivity gives native visible and first nonvisible endpoint classes. A local root-sheet theorem derives these objects from the same observation model. The finite-sheet tensor envelope and the efficient Gaussian interpretation are retained with their full proofs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A singular observation contains more metric information than its order of
vanishing. Its normalized residual image records the directions in which
that order is attained. Minimizing over free coordinates and over invisible
one-sided coordinates then produces two different quotients. A structural
theory must explain both how these objects arise and what can be recovered
from them.

Our first result concerns the radius used in a finite-map reduction.
For $R=(F-a,G-b)$ let $c$ be the largest centre-sheet residual and let $M$
be the supremum of the second derivative of $G$ in fast $F$ coordinates.
Theorem~\ref{thm:residual-scale} shows that the relevant error is $cM$,
not $rM$, when $c=o(r)$. Thus a bounded radius-scaled derivative error
need not destroy affine asymptotics. Theorem~\ref{thm:divisorial} computes
the exact certificate from the determinant of $DF$ and the first two
polar numerators on proper real charts. Proposition~\ref{prop:nonlinear-wall}
gives the nonlinear residual and its variational cost when residual-scale
smallness actually fails. The earlier finite-map theorem, its coupled
weighted examples, and its tensor-envelope representation are included
without deleting their hypotheses or proofs.

The second result identifies what survives the first loss of endpoint
visibility. In endpoint dimension at least three, removing any one open
active cell still leaves enough rank and incidence information to
reconstruct every minimal representation
(Theorem~\ref{thm:one-missing}). In dimension two there is an extra scalar
modulus, and Theorem~\ref{thm:three-cells} gives the full fibre in each
of the three possible order types. A separate rank-one calculation
explains when Hessians coalesce and why deletion of individual coordinates
can miss a lower-dimensional representation. These are continuations of
the fully visible reconstruction theorem, not substitutions of smaller
claims for that theorem.

The third result derives a native class from binary observations with
calibrated channels and polynomial component kernels. The clocks and root
factors are chosen independently of any desired quotient. At the minimally
identifying clock count, Lagrange interpolation determines the dual score
columns and forces $Q^{-1}$ to be congruent to a finite Hankel moment cone
(Theorem~\ref{thm:binary-hankel}). This gives an exact all-dimensional
classification for the stated exposure family, including component
colours. In the one-colour subclass total positivity yields full endpoint
visibility for one endpoint with at least two interior clusters and for
two endpoints with at least three. Two interior clusters and two endpoints
give precisely the missing-empty three-cell class. Thus the first
nonvisible quotient occurs in the original observation mechanism rather
than in a separately designed quadratic form. Theorem~\ref{thm:unification}
identifies the same model's finite root quotient, affine observation sheet,
native information, and endpoint profile.

\subsection*{Relation to earlier work}
The geometric foundations are classical. Semialgebraic selection and
one-variable preparation are used as in \cite{BCR}. Proper real
uniformization and the normal-crossings theorem are used in the precise
forms of \cite[Theorem 0.1 and Corollary 4.9]{BM}. Our claim is not a new
resolution theorem. It is the residual-scale comparison, together with
an exact application to the numerator ideals $P$ and $N_2$ which preserves
cancellations and separates radius boundaries from residual boundaries.
The accessibility proof is included because a complex divisor or an
unreachable real face would not certify the stated uniform limit.

Schur complements, convex duality and quadratic minimization are standard
\cite{BV}. Parametric quadratic programming provides the polyhedral
active regions and their quadratic value functions; see
\cite{BMDP,BMDPc}. We use that forward description. The inverse problem
here is different: the active-set labels and even the endpoint count are
not supplied. The fully visible Hessian lattice, its one-deleted-vertex
continuation, and the scalar three-cell fibres concern recovery from the
labelled value function on the retained cone. The distinction between
coordinate redundancy and minimal representability is essential at rank
loss.

Information matrices as weighted sums of regression tensors belong to
classical design theory, including \cite{KW}. Finite moment spaces and
their design-theoretic role are treated in \cite{DS}. Cauchy determinants,
complementary minors and total positivity are classical matrix tools;
\cite{Pinkus} gives a systematic account. We do not claim these identities
or the moment cone as new constructions. The model-specific step is that
the binary root scores force the interpolation columns in
\eqref{eq:cauchy-columns}; nuisance elimination then places the
\emph{inverse} information, not generally the information itself, in
that cone. This distinction leads to separate exact secant statements
and prevents transferring a dual-query count to a different oracle.

Latent-model identifiability has an extensive literature. For example,
\cite{AMR} derives broad generic identifiability results from tensor
structure in models with many observed variables. The calibrated clock
family here has a different mechanism: scalar log-odds interpolation at
distinct clocks, with a completely displayed coefficient inverse. We
retain the unknown-channel binary inverse separately and do not equate
these two experiments. Finally, comparison by randomizations
\cite{Blackwell}, local asymptotic normality and efficient information
\cite{vdV} supply the statistical language. The finite-alphabet proof
below identifies the particular quotient cost and root scaling; it does
not turn exact evaluation queries into independent samples.

\subsection*{Organization}
Section~\ref{sec:categories} fixes the objects and morphisms before they
are used. Sections~\ref{sec:reduction}--\ref{sec:tensors} derive residual
quotients; Sections~\ref{sec:endpoints}--\ref{sec:walls} reconstruct
endpoint profiles. The paired native benchmark is retained in
Section~\ref{sec:native}; Sections~\ref{sec:binary-hankel}--\ref{sec:unification}
derive the binary class and its statistical meaning. The appendices
supply real accessibility, the two expanded proof transitions, the precise
clock-pair quantifier, and the relation to the complete preserved record.
The strict hypotheses of the different theorems are not conflated.
