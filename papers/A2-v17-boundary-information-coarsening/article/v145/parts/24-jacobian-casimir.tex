\section{A Jacobian identity for the contraction operator}
\label{sec:jacobian-casimir}

The contraction theorem admits an operator-level interpretation that
also determines its singular values. This interpretation distinguishes
the exterior-valued map from classical differential operators while
identifying an exact composite with them. It gives a second proof of
the singular-rank formula without a descent argument. The proofs in
Section~\ref{sec:uniform-support} remain valid independently.

Let \(D=\det V\), \(\dim V=n\ge2\), and \(W=\Sym^2V\).
Use the normalization of \(j_n\) in \eqref{eq:uniform-j}, and let
\[
 C_n:\bigwedge^n W\longrightarrow D\otimes\Sym^nV,
 \qquad C_n(w_1\wedge\cdots\wedge w_n)
 =\epsilon\otimes\det(\partial_{x_j}w_i).
\]
For \(h\in\Sym^2V\) and \(q\in\Sym^2V^*\), write
\(\varepsilon_h(z)=h\wedge z\), \(\Delta_q=q(\partial)\),
and let \(\mathcal D_{hq}\) be the derivation of \(\Sym V\)
induced by \(hq:V\to V\). For symmetric matrices \(H,Q\),
\[
 h=x^tHx,\qquad \Delta_q=\sum_{i,j}Q_{ij}\partial_{x_i}\partial_{x_j},
 \qquad\mathcal D_{hq}=\sum_{i,j}(HQ)_{ij}x_i\partial_{x_j}.
\]
Off-diagonal entries are counted twice in both quadratic expressions.

\begin{theorem}[Mixed Jacobian--contraction identity]
\label{thm:mixed-jacobian-contraction}
For arbitrary \(h\in\Sym^2V\), \(q\in\Sym^2V^*\), and
\(f\in\Sym^nV\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed-jacobian-contraction}
 C_n\varepsilon_h\kappa_{n,q}(\epsilon\otimes f)
 =\epsilon\otimes\left(\frac4n\mathcal D_{hq}f
       -\frac{2}{n(n-1)}h\Delta_q f\right).
\end{equation}
No nondegeneracy hypothesis on either form is required.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
It suffices to take \(f=\ell^n\), since powers span \(\Sym^nV\).
Write \(\ell=a^tx\). The Jacobian matrix of the ordered quadrics
\(\ell x_1,\ldots,\ell x_n\) is \(J=\ell I+xa^t\).
The contraction coefficients are the entries of \(b=Qa\).
Inserting \(h\) after contraction gives the sum of determinants
obtained by replacing row \(i\) of \(J\) by \(2x^tH\),
with coefficient \(b_i\). The contraction sign is cancelled by
moving the inserted first factor back to position \(i\).
On the open set \(\ell\ne0\),
\[
 \det J=2\ell^n,\qquad
 J^{-1}=\ell^{-1}I-\frac{xa^t}{2\ell^2}.
\]
The row-replacement formula evaluates that sum as
\[
 2\ell^n(2x^tH)J^{-1}Qa
 =4\ell^{n-1}x^tHQa-2\ell^{n-2}(x^tHx)(a^tQa).
\]
But \(\mathcal D_{hq}\ell^n=n\ell^{n-1}x^tHQa\) and
\(\Delta_q\ell^n=n(n-1)(a^tQa)\ell^{n-2}\).
Both sides are polynomials, so the equality holds everywhere,
including isotropic powers and singular matrices.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The singular kernel from differential identities]
\label{cor:singular-kernel-mixed}
If \(0<\rank q=r<n\), then
\[
 \ker\kappa_{n,q}=D\otimes\Sym^n(\rad q),\qquad
 \dim\ker\kappa_{n,q}=\binom{2n-r-1}{n}.
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Suppose \(\kappa_{n,q}(\epsilon\otimes f)=0\). Choose
\(0\ne b\in\rad q\) and take \(h=b^2\) in
\eqref{eq:mixed-jacobian-contraction}. Since \(hq=0\),
we obtain \(b^2\Delta_qf=0\), hence \(\Delta_qf=0\).
Thus \(\mathcal D_{hq}f=0\) for every \(h\). Choose
coordinates with \(q=\diag(I_r,0)\), and take \(h=x_i^2\)
for \(i\le r\). Then \(x_i\partial_{x_i}f=0\), so
\(f\) involves only radical variables. Conversely,
\(j_n(\epsilon\otimes b^n)\) is killed by contraction for
\(b\in\rad q\). Polarization proves the reverse inclusion,
and counting monomials gives the dimension. The differential argument
uses characteristic zero; the diagonalization is over \(\C\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{The angular Casimir and exact singular values}

For nondegenerate \(q\), set \(\rho=q^{-1}\),
\(E=\sum x_i\partial_{x_i}\), and
\[
 \mathscr C_q=E(E+n-2)-\rho\Delta_q.
\]
This is the angular Casimir with nonnegative harmonic eigenvalues.
The harmonic decomposition is classical; see
\cite[Theorem~9 and \S4(a)]{HoweClassical}. The following identity
identifies the particular exterior operator:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jacobian-casimir-composite}
 C_n\varepsilon_{\rho}\kappa_{n,q}
 =\frac{2}{n(n-1)}(1_D\otimes\mathscr C_q)
 \quad\hbox{on }D\otimes\Sym^nV.
\end{equation}
Indeed \(\mathcal D_{\rho q}=E\), and \(E=n\) on this degree.

For the metric statement, take \(q=I\) in a specified real
orthonormal frame and complexify. Equip the tensor, symmetric and
exterior powers with their induced positive Hermitian products, and
\(D\) with unit volume. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalized-fischer-metric}
 \|x^\alpha\|^2=\frac{\alpha!}{k!}\quad(|\alpha|=k),
 \qquad \|x_ix_j\|^2=\tfrac12\quad(i\ne j).
\end{equation}
This is the degree-normalized Fischer product. Compare the unnormalized
convention in \cite[Definition~6.1 and Lemma~6.2]{DeBieEelbodeRoels}.

\begin{lemma}[Adjoint normalization]
\label{lem:jacobian-adjoint}
For these products, \(j_n^*=2^{-n}C_n\) and
\(\iota_q^*=\varepsilon_{\rho}\). Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:contraction-gram-casimir}
 \kappa_{n,q}^*\kappa_{n,q}
 =\frac{2^{1-n}}{n(n-1)}(1_D\otimes\mathscr C_q).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The Riesz representative of \(q\) in \(W\) is \(\rho\),
so the exterior adjoint formula gives the second assertion. For the
first, test against \(\epsilon\otimes\ell^n\) and a wedge
\(w_1\wedge\cdots\wedge w_n\). The quadratic product pairs
\(\ell x_i\) with \(w_j\) as one half of the corresponding
first derivative evaluated at \(\ell\), with the usual Hermitian
conjugation. The determinant contributes \(2^{-n}\).
The normalized symmetric product pairs \(\ell^n\) with a
polynomial by evaluation. Thus the determinant of derivatives is the
required adjoint. Powers and decomposable wedges span. Composition
with \eqref{eq:jacobian-casimir-composite} proves the last formula.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Singular-value decomposition and a sharp inverse bound]
\label{thm:contraction-singular-values}
Under the preceding normalization, the squared singular value on
\(D\otimes\rho^{(n-m)/2}\mathcal H_m\), for
\(m=n,n-2,\ldots\), is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:contraction-singular-values}
 \sigma_m^2=\frac{2^{1-n}m(m+n-2)}{n(n-1)}.
\end{equation}
Its multiplicity is
\(\binom{n+m-1}{m}-\binom{n+m-3}{m-2}\), with the second
term zero for \(m<2\). A zero singular value occurs precisely
when \(n\) is even, with eigenspace
\(D\otimes\C\rho^{n/2}\). On the orthogonal complement
of the kernel,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:contraction-condition-number}
 \operatorname{cond}(\kappa_{n,q})=
 \begin{cases}\sqrt{2n},&n\text{ odd},\\
 \sqrt{n-1},&n\text{ even}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Let \(\mathscr C_q^\dagger\) be its inverse on the positive
harmonic summands and zero on the scalar summand. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:contraction-pseudoinverse}
 \kappa_{n,q}^{\dagger}
 =\frac{n(n-1)}2(1_D\otimes\mathscr C_q^{\dagger})
 C_n\varepsilon_{\rho}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For \(h_m\in\mathcal H_m\), differentiation gives
\(\Delta_q(\rho^t h_m)=2t(2m+2t+n-2)\rho^{t-1}h_m\).
With \(m+2t=n\), the Casimir eigenvalue is \(m(m+n-2)\).
Equation~\eqref{eq:contraction-gram-casimir} proves the formula and
kills precisely the scalar line. The dimension follows from the
surjectivity of the Laplacian in the harmonic decomposition.
The largest Casimir eigenvalue is \(2n(n-1)\); the smallest
positive one is \(n-1\) in odd degree and \(2n\) in even
degree. Their ratios give the condition numbers, attained on the
nonzero endpoint harmonic summands. Finally use
\(A^\dagger=(A^*A)^\dagger A^*\) and
\(\kappa_{n,q}^*=2^{-n}C_n\varepsilon_{\rho}\).
\end{proof}

The kernel statements, unlike the metric assertions, are invariant
under arbitrary complex changes of basis. The two arguments above
therefore independently prove Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform-contraction}.
The condition number refers to the specified compact real form and
induced norms; it is not invariant under \(O(n,\C)\), and no
bound for arbitrary ill-conditioned coordinate changes is asserted.
The mixed identity explains why comparison of codomains alone was
insufficient: the exterior operator is not the apolar Laplacian, but
its specified Jacobian composite is exactly a classical angular
operator.
