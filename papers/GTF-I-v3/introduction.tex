\section{Introduction}\label{sec:v3-introduction}
A finite representation of a prediction has to satisfy two requirements.
It must resolve the law produced by the observations, and it must support
future updates without recovering observations that have been discarded.
These requirements need not have the same quantitative cost. A law may be
one-dimensional and unchanged at every time while the number of labels
needed to retain its predictions depends on how unresolved information
is transported between acquisitions.

The principal result makes this distinction sharp. Consider a variable
in $[0,1)$ which is reset to an observed uniform value with probability
$p$ and otherwise follows the doubling map, with no new observation.
After each update only one of $M$ labels remains available. A terminal
prediction is requested after the label has been formed. The acquired
state is uniform at every checkpoint, so its optimal checkpoint squared
error is exactly $1/(12M^2)$. Nevertheless, writing $q=1-p$, we prove
\[
 \inf_{\text{causal $M$-label machines}}\sup_{t\ge0}
       \E|X_t-\widehat X_t|^2
 \asymp_q
 \begin{cases}
 M^{-2},&q<1/4,\\
 (1+\log M)M^{-2},&q=1/4,\\
 M^{-\log(1/q)/\log2},&q>1/4.
 \end{cases}
\]
The lower bound includes randomized, nonstationary machines, not just
state quantizers. The upper construction is a binary-suffix machine;
its labels include the suffix length, and its program does not know the
reset probability. Thus the change in rate is not an artifact of a
particular grid, an uncharged age register, or a known real calibration.

The proof uses a general regenerative converse,
Theorem~\ref{thm:v3-orbit}. If $T$ is the evolution between acquisitions
and $f$ the predictive readout, the relevant acquired object is
\[
 u\longmapsto
 \bigl(\sqrt{1-q}\,q^{k/2}f(T^ku)\bigr)_{k\ge0}\in\ell^2.
\]
One label at a reset must resolve this entire weighted future orbit.
A renewal decomposition turns its $M$-centre quantization error into a
lower bound for every causal implementation. For the doubling map, the
orbit metric changes its small-scale order when $4q$ crosses one. The
critical logarithm is forced by the accumulation of all scales, not
postulated through a stability hypothesis. Theorem~\ref{thm:v3-phase}
proves the resulting all-integer-budget law.

A second result addresses statistical uncertainty before compression.
In the paired experiment
\[
 Y=f(\theta)+\eta+\varepsilon_Y,\qquad
 W=\eta+\varepsilon_W,
\]
$\eta$ is an unrestricted unknown additive calibration, and the joint
Gaussian measurement noise may be correlated. For every bounded loss
and every label budget, Theorem~\ref{thm:v3-quotient} identifies its
minimax risk for $\theta$ exactly with that of the observed difference
$Y-W$. The proof uses an increasingly diffuse nuisance distribution and
an explicit parameter-independent simulation. It does not mistake a
nuisance-dependent conditional law for a sufficient-statistic kernel.
When the difference mean has a two-sided contact geometry in its noise
metric, Theorem~\ref{thm:v3-contact} gives matching statistical and
representation bounds. The mean map may be nonlinear and nonseparable;
the constants are displayed independently of the contact orders.

The two results address different losses. The regenerative theorem
measures excess relative to the complete acquisition history. The
calibration theorem measures error relative to the unknown parameter.
Together with the general causal-resolution theorem below, they identify
three distinct objects: the law actually acquired, the evolution of
unresolved predictions, and the statistical separation of the underlying
parameters. None may be replaced merely by an ambient coordinate count.

\subsection{Relation to previous work}
Predictive-state representations and causal states describe the information
required for future prediction
\cite{ShaliziCrutchfield2001,LittmanSuttonSingh2002,BarnettCrutchfield2015}.
Predictive rate-distortion theory already studies lossy representations of
future-relevant information \cite{MarzenCrutchfield2016}, while metric
representations of predictive states include Cantor embeddings
\cite{LoomisCrutchfield2022}. Accordingly, the use of future predictions
as a representation is not our novelty claim. The new assertion here is
the regenerative lower bound for a hard persistent-cardinality constraint
and its matching causal implementation with the critical logarithmic law.
Neither an information-rate constraint nor a codebook for one checkpoint
alone specifies that implementation problem.

Quantization of nonlinear filters has a substantial existing theory
\cite{PagesPham2005,KalogeriasPetropulu2016}. Finite approximations of
belief decision processes and finite-memory feedback policies are studied
in \cite{SaldiYukselLinder2017,KaraYuksel2022,KBY2024,DKY2024}.
In particular, Kara--Y\"uksel \cite[Theorem 12]{KaraYuksel2022} compares
an implemented finite-window policy with an unrestricted optimal policy
under its stated filter-stability and discount assumptions.
Subramanian--Sinha--Seraj--Mahajan \cite[Theorems 9 and 27]{SSSM2022}
derive value and policy bounds from approximate information states.
Our Theorem~\ref{thm:v2-control} is an explicit finite-reference
specialization in this established direction, not the first
near-optimality theorem for partially observed control. By contrast,
Theorem~\ref{thm:v3-phase} supplies a matching lower bound over all
persistent $M$-label machines in an expanding experiment; it is not a
consequence of a common-controller simulation estimate.

The A1 attainable-geometry theorem \cite{QiA1} separates a global cover,
actually acquired mass, and causal compatibility. Its finite-horizon
anisotropic conclusion is recovered in Corollary~\ref{cor:v2-a1}.
The present orbit converse concerns the additional cost of transporting
unresolved information over arbitrarily long intervals. In particular,
the checkpoint law in Theorem~\ref{thm:v3-phase} cannot distinguish
any of its three regimes. The contact experiments of \cite{QiA2}
provide model-specific singular local statistics. Our paired-measurement
results join a measured nuisance, the noise metric, a contact modulus,
and a hard label constraint in one experiment. They preserve the distinction
between additive measurement noise and a score computed from correlated
raw data. The general relation between statistical moduli and rates is
classical \cite{DonohoLiu1991}; the quantitative conclusions here concern
the precisely specified constrained experiment.

\subsection{Organization and conventions}
Sections~\ref{sec:v3-regeneration}--\ref{sec:v3-calibration} contain
the future-orbit converse, the sharp regenerative transition, and the
correlated calibration and contact theorems. Sections
\ref{sec:v2-resolution}--\ref{sec:v2-control} develop acquired-law
resolution, nonuniform stability, every-positive-refresh hidden Markov
experiments, singular acquisition, and finite-reference control.
Section~\ref{sec:v2-resources} specifies the distinction between
persistent labels, acquisition, program description, and numerical
workspace. The foundational statements and boundary examples are given
with their full proofs in the appendices.

All lower bounds state which law is acquired and what randomness is
independent of it. All causal constructions specify their persistent
registers. A bound on $\sup_t\E L_t$ is not a bound on
$\E\sup_t L_t$. Small total-variation errors are not substituted for
exponential equivalence in large-deviation problems. These distinctions
also govern the interfaces with the preceding theta manuscripts; no
spectral, particle, kinetic, or unbounded-operator theorem is imported
from a declaration of pipeline completion.
