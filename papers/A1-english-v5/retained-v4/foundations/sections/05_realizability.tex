\section{Attainable selections and restricted preparations}\label{sec:realizability}
\subsection{The reference law and the protocol class are different objects}
Fix a base experiment with path law $P$. A declared protocol class $\mathfrak A_b$ has a resource budget $b$ and produces a set of path laws $\mathfrak R_b$. This set must be derived from actual preparation and intervention operations. For the information comparison in this subsection retain only its laws absolutely continuous with respect to $P$, and assume at least one has finite entropy. Define
\[
 \Psi_b^{\rm real}(V)=\sup_{Q\in\mathfrak R_b}\{QV-D(Q\Vert P)\}.
\]
This definition prices information by relative entropy. It does not identify that price with the mechanical resource $b$.

\begin{theorem}[Exact realizability deficit]\label{thm:real-gap}
For bounded $V$,
\begin{equation}
 \log Pe^V-\Psi_b^{\rm real}(V)
 =\inf_{Q\in\mathfrak R_b}D(Q\Vert Q^V).
 \label{eq:real-gap}
\end{equation}
If $Q^V\in\mathfrak R_b$, the deficit is zero. If the deficit is zero and its infimum is attained, an attaining law is $Q^V$. Without attainment, zero deficit means approximation in relative entropy, hence in total variation, and does not imply exact implementation.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:gibbs-gap} holds for every competitor. Taking its infimum proves \eqref{eq:real-gap}; the finite-entropy competitor and bounded $V$ make the relevant supremum finite. Entropy is zero exactly when the two probabilities coincide. Pinsker's inequality, proved in Appendix~\ref{app:measure}, turns a sequence of vanishing relative entropies into convergence in total variation. Membership of the limit in $\mathfrak R_b$ is an additional closure issue.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Formal preparation pullback versus implementation}
If a fixed deterministic experiment is $P=\Gamma_\#\mu$, then
\[
 d\mu^V(z)=\frac{e^{V(\Gamma(z))}}{\mu(e^{V\circ\Gamma})}\,d\mu(z)
\]
has $\Gamma_\#\mu^V=Q^V$, by testing against bounded path functions. This is a mathematical pullback identity. Whether a device can prepare $\mu^V$, resolve its microstates, or evaluate its selection rule is not settled by the identity. The accessible preparation class below makes a particular restriction explicit.

\subsection{A complete solution for coarse preparation}
Let $S:\Omega\to X$ be an initially available statistic. In a deterministic fixed-protocol experiment it can be an initial-state statistic transported to path space. The admissible preparation laws are
\[
 \mathfrak R_S=\{Q=q(S)P:q\ge0,\ E_Pq(S)=1\}.
\]
These laws can change the marginal of $S$, but preserve the conditional distribution of the unresolved path given $S$. Set $m(S)=E_P[V\mid S]$.

\begin{theorem}[Coarse-preparation optimum and loss]\label{thm:coarse-prep}
For bounded $V$,
\begin{equation}
 \sup_{Q\in\mathfrak R_S}\{QV-D(Q\Vert P)\}
 =\log E_Pe^{m(S)},
 \qquad
 dQ_S^*=\frac{e^{m(S)}}{E_Pe^m}dP.
 \label{eq:coarse-opt}
\end{equation}
The optimizer is unique. The deficit relative to unrestricted selection is
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_S(V)=\log Pe^V-\log Pe^{E(V\mid S)}
 =D(Q_S^*\Vert Q^V)\ge0.
 \label{eq:coarse-deficit}
\end{equation}
It vanishes exactly when $V$ is $\sigma(S)$-measurable modulo $P$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $Q=q(S)P$, conditional expectation gives $QV=E[q(S)m(S)]$, while $D(Q\Vert P)=E[q(S)\log q(S)]$. Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:gibbs} on the $S$ marginal. This proves the value and unique density in \eqref{eq:coarse-opt}. Substitution into \eqref{eq:gibbs-gap} gives the equality in \eqref{eq:coarse-deficit}. Conditional Jensen gives $E(e^V\mid S)\ge e^m$. Equality of the two outer integrals forces equality almost everywhere because the pointwise difference is nonnegative. Strict convexity of the exponential makes $V$ conditionally constant. Conversely conditional constancy makes the two tilted laws identical.
\end{proof}

The effective source when \emph{observing an already tilted experiment} is $\log E(e^V\mid S)$, not $m$. The conditional mean appears in \eqref{eq:coarse-opt} because the permitted \emph{preparation operation} cannot alter hidden conditional laws. These two operations must not be confused.

\begin{proposition}[Nested preparation and the local information loss]\label{prop:coarse-expansion}
If $S_1$ is a measurable function of $S_2$, then
$\Delta_{S_2}(V)\le\Delta_{S_1}(V)$. For bounded $F$,
\[
 \Delta_S(tF)=\tfrac12t^2E\Var(F\mid S)+O_{\norm F_\infty}(|t|^3).
\]
For fixed $P,S$ and a $C^1$ bounded-source family $a\mapsto V_a$ in the supremum norm,
\[
 \partial_a\Delta_S(V_a)
 =Q^{V_a}\dot V_a-Q_{S,a}^*E_P[\dot V_a\mid S].
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The coarse preparation classes are nested, so their variational values are ordered. Taylor expansion gives
$\log Ee^{tF}=tEF+t^2\Var(F)/2+O(t^3)$ uniformly for bounded $F$. Apply the same expansion to $E(F\mid S)$ and use total variance. Differentiation under the bounded exponential and the contraction of conditional expectation in $L^\infty$ give the final formula. It concerns fixed reference $P$ and fixed $S$; varying geometry requires the additional response terms in Section~\ref{sec:response}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A density cap and an explicit optimizer}
A practical preparation restriction is a bound on density amplification. For $M>1$ let
\[
 \mathfrak R_{S,M}=\{q(S)P:0\le q\le M,\ E q=1\}.
\]
Such a law has $D(Q\Vert P)\le\log M$, but $M$ need not be a mechanical energy bound.

\begin{theorem}[Capped coarse preparation]\label{thm:cap}
For bounded $m=E(V\mid S)$, the unique optimizer of
$E[qm-q\log q]$ over $\mathfrak R_{S,M}$ is
\begin{equation}
 q_M(S)=\min\{M,c e^{m(S)}\},\qquad E\min\{M,ce^m\}=1.
 \label{eq:cap}
\end{equation}
The positive scalar $c$ is unique for $M>1$. For $M=1$, the sole feasible law is $P$. The value deficit is $D(q_MP\Vert Q^V)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The function $F(c)=E\min(M,ce^m)$ is continuous, equals zero at zero, and is $M>1$ for all sufficiently large $c$. At a solution with value one, the unsaturated set has positive measure, so $F$ is strictly increasing across that level. This proves existence and uniqueness of $c$.
For $\phi_x(q)=qm(x)-q\log q$, its derivative at $q_M$ equals $-\log c-1$ on the unsaturated set and is at least that constant on the saturated set. Concavity gives
\[
 \phi_x(q)-\phi_x(q_M)\le\phi_x'(q_M)(q-q_M).
\]
On the saturated set $q-q_M\le0$; elsewhere the derivative is the constant. Integrating gives an upper bound $(-\log c-1)E(q-q_M)=0$. Strict concavity gives uniqueness. The gap formula is again \eqref{eq:gibbs-gap}. The case $M=1$ follows from $q\le1$ and $Eq=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[First-order response of the capped optimum]\label{prop:cap-response}
Keep $P,S,M>1$ fixed. For bounded $m,\widetilde m$, the optimal densities satisfy
\[
 d_{\TV}(q_mP,q_{\widetilde m}P)\le\tfrac12\norm{m-\widetilde m}_\infty.
\]
If $a\mapsto m_a$ is $C^1$ in $L^\infty$, its optimal value is $C^1$ with derivative $E[q_{m_a}\dot m_a]$. No classical second derivative is claimed across changes of the active cap set.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The two first-order optimality inequalities, added with opposite competitors, give
\[
 D(q_mP\Vert q_{\widetilde m}P)+D(q_{\widetilde m}P\Vert q_mP)
 \le E[(q_m-q_{\widetilde m})(m-\widetilde m)].
\]
Both densities are bounded away from zero for fixed bounded $m,\widetilde m$, so all terms are finite. If $d$ is their TV distance, Pinsker bounds the left side by $4d^2$ from below, and the right side is at most $2d\norm{m-\widetilde m}_\infty$. This proves the bound, including $d=0$. Comparing the old and new optimizers bounds the change in optimal value between $E[q_m\delta m]$ and $E[q_{m+\delta m}\delta m]$. Their difference is $O(\norm{\delta m}_\infty^2)$. Divide by the parameter increment and use the TV bound to obtain the derivative and its continuity.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Causal acceptance and its exact trial count}
\begin{proposition}[What rejection sampling implements]\label{prop:acceptance}
Suppose a trial from $P$ and a uniform $U\in[0,1]$ are independently prepared. For bounded $V\le M_0$, accept the completed trial when $U\le e^{V-M_0}$. The accepted path law is $Q^V$, with success probability $e^{-M_0}Pe^V$. If independent trials can actually be repeated, the number of trials to first acceptance is geometric with mean $e^{M_0}/Pe^V$ and failure probability $(1-e^{-M_0}Pe^V)^k$ after $k$ trials.

If $q(S)\le M$ and $Eq=1$, an initially available statistic $S$ permits acceptance before running the subsequent experiment, using $U\le q(S)/M$. The accepted preparation is $q(S)P$ and success probability is $1/M$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For any event $A$, independence gives
$P(A,\mathrm{accept})=e^{-M_0}E[\one_Ae^V]$.
Normalize to obtain $Q^V$. Independent repeated successes yield the geometric product and its elementary mean. Replacing the weight by $q(S)$ gives the second assertion. The availability of $S$ before the trajectory is precisely what makes preselection causal. A source depending on a later collision can instead be evaluated after the trial; its postselection does not change the already executed forces.
\end{proof}

A prepared rotor with uniform angular phase can supply a uniform auxiliary coordinate for a deterministic threshold operation. This specifies a statistical apparatus preparation, not a proof of unlimited precision or zero cost. Evaluating a complicated acceptance function, rejecting a particle, resetting a device, and storing a record have their own resource costs. The trial-count theorem does not price them.

\subsection{Why finite mechanical work need not have finite path entropy}
Take $\ddot x=0$ and $\ddot x=u\ne0$ on $[0,T]$, with the same bounded initial preparation. On full $C^2$ paths the two laws are supported on disjoint acceleration constraints. Their relative entropy is infinite. Yet the kinetic-energy change under constant force is finite for bounded initial velocities and finite $T$. Thus intervention may leave the absolute-continuity class of the reference law. Formula~\eqref{eq:real-gap} applies to its declared dominated branch; mechanically achievable singular laws require direct comparison or an observation-level common model.

Even when an admissible set $\mathfrak R_b$ is fixed, a value $\sup_{Q\in\mathfrak R_b}\{QF-C(Q)\}$ is convex in $F$, being a supremum of affine functions. Realizability restrictions alone do not create arbitrary nonconvex dependence on a payoff. A desired nonconvex branch must identify its additional mechanism.
