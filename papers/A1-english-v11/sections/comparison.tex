\subsection{Relation to finite approximation and information states}
Finite approximation of a controlled model and finite realization of a
prediction state are related, but their conclusions and numerical inputs
must be distinguished. We make the comparison at the level of particular
statements, rather than regarding finite-state approximation as a new
principle.

Saldi, Y\"uksel and Linder \cite[Theorem 5.3]{SaldiYukselLinder}
bound the discounted performance loss of policies obtained from finite
approximating Markov decision processes by $O(n^{-1/d})$. The result
uses compact Euclidean state space, a Lipschitz cost, a transition kernel
Lipschitz in the Wasserstein distance, and $\beta K_2<1$. Their finite
model uses state quantization and averaged transition kernels; their
Section 6 also studies order optimality. Our theorem does not strengthen
that discounted-control conclusion. Here the supplied data are certified
evaluations of feasible histories; the output is an integer transition
program with at most a prescribed $M$ labels at each stage. The scale
selected in Theorem~\ref{thm:adaptive-compiler} is the attainable
covering radius itself, not a prescribed ambient-dimensional power.
The collision law additionally identifies that scale and supplies a
matching prediction converse for the same experiment.

Kara and Y\"uksel \cite[Assumption 4 and Theorem 12]{KaraYuksel}
obtain an exponentially decreasing discounted policy-loss bound for
finite-window feedback after a warm-up period. Their hypotheses include
transition regularity, a domination condition, a Dobrushin-type filter
stability coefficient $\alpha<1$, and a restriction on the discount.
The finite-window description retains recent observations and actions.
Our finite-horizon construction does not require filter contraction,
but makes no corresponding infinite-horizon assertion. It enumerates
histories only offline and retains an index, not a recent observation
window. Its effective input is a finite coefficient--moment table in
the monomial case. Window length, persistent-label budget, and numerical
program length are different resources; no comparison of their optima
follows simply by identifying these names.

Subramanian, Sinha, Seraj and Mahajan
\cite[Definition 7 and Theorem 9]{Subramanian} give an all-history
value approximation and policy-loss bound from reward and transition
errors of an approximate information state. Their recursive error
allowance combines reward error, an integral-probability-metric transition
error, and the next-stage allowance; the policy bound has twice that
allowance. They also treat action compression in Theorem 17. Thus
neither an all-history error recurrence nor action compression is new
here. The additional conclusion proved here is a finite synthesis with
a prescribed label budget: approximate feasible histories give a
computable two-sided covering certificate, which selects its own
numerical mesh. In the monomial experiment the certificate is tied to
the full collision-sensitive attainable profile by
Lemma~\ref{lem:cover-profile}, with both the finite program and its
numerical advice separately accounted for.

These distinctions concern the displayed theorems, not an assertion that
one of the cited theories is contained in another. The classical
farthest-first estimate supplies the finite certificate; the attained
posterior geometry supplies its interpretation as the sharp prediction
scale. Their combination yields the resource phases of
Corollary~\ref{cor:resource-phases} without an input identifying which
phase is present. The geometric and numerical assertions require separate
proofs, and the specializations in the appendices retain their own
hypotheses.
