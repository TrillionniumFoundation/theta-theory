\section{The inverse autoconvolution problem}
\label{sec:v10-deautoconvolution}
After the physical relative law has been proved, the inverse in
Section~\ref{sec:v9-compatibility} belongs to the theory of nonlinear
Volterra equations. The passage from a billiard record to that equation,
and the topology of its inversion, must be distinguished from the
convolution machinery itself.

Dai and Lamm~\cite{DaiLamm} develop local regularization for inverse
autoconvolution, preserving the causal structure and proving convergence
for continuous and $L^2$ data. Their work supplies the relevant
regularization context. We do not claim that causal inversion or a
regularization principle is new, or apply an unverified theorem from
that setting directly to the singular density here.

Hofmann, Werner and Deng~\cite{HofmannWernerDeng} consider real
$L^2$ functions supported in a cube, with full or limited convolution
data. Their limited-data uniqueness result uses nonnegativity and
support reaching the origin; it belongs to a convolution-support
framework. Our normalized kernel $k(x)=x^{-1/2}V(x)$ has support at
the origin and is locally integrable, but is not in $L^2$ there:
$V(0)=1$ implies $\int_0^a k(x)^2\,\dd x=\infty$.
Thus neither an $L^2$ uniqueness theorem nor $L^2$ ill-posedness is
silently substituted for the statements proved here.

There are four specific features of the present inverse. First, the
observed scalar law is $F=T(V,V)$, with two residual integrations
between $F$ and $k*k=(\pi/2)(d^2F)''$. Second, normalization fixes
the leading singularity of $k$, so the difference of two candidate
kernels is continuous at zero and Abel reduction gives a forced
second-kind equation. Third, all convolutions are restricted to a
finite collar: reconstruction at energy $d$ uses no values beyond
$d$, no full-line Fourier inversion and no analytic continuation.
Fourth, the deterministic stability statement uses $C^3$ law error
and uniform profile loss. It is a full-function statement for smooth
profiles, rather than a determination of their Taylor series.

These features identify the exact functional setting of
Theorem~\ref{thm:v9-profile-stability}; they are not a claim of a new
general deautoconvolution method. Its geometric content is the
physical realization of the singular weighted energy measure and the
relative estimate that makes it observable. Its statistical use
requires another step. Section~\ref{sec:v10-acquisition} regularizes
finite scalar data on a positive-node grid, retaining the preparation
cost of the rare selected event. This uses a bounded smoothness class;
it does not replace $C^3$ by $C^0$ in the unregularized theorem.
The exponent obtained there is sufficient and is not asserted to
be the optimal derivative loss or minimax exponent. Nor is an arbitrary
regularization candidate asserted to be a realizable contact graph.

The Abel norm in Section~\ref{sec:v11-abel} is a norm of integrated
flux differences with zero initial value and slope; on arbitrary flux
functions it has an affine nullspace. Its two-sided estimate is on the
image of the normalized positive profile map. It does not assert that
every scalar law is an autoconvolution square. The refinement in the
observation theorem uses this linear discrepancy together with a
globally smooth reconstruction and a different variance allocation.
The subsequent calibration argument uses the physical identity that an
onset error translates the integrated flux. These are specified
consequences for this billiard invariant, not claims that Abel inversion,
local polynomial regularization or Bernoulli concentration are new.
