\section{Mechanical response and moving-domain currents}\label{sec:response}
\subsection{Where a score formula is legitimate}
Suppose $a\mapsto P_a$ is $C^1$ in signed-measure variation, $V_a,F_a$ are $C^1$ in the supremum norm, and $Z_a=P_ae^{V_a}>0$. Let $Q_a=e^{V_a}P_a/Z_a$ and $U_a=Q_aF_a$.

\begin{theorem}[Separated preparation, source, and observable response]\label{thm:regular-response}
\begin{equation}
 U_a'=Q_a\dot F_a+\Cov_{Q_a}(F_a,\dot V_a)
 +Z_a^{-1}\dot P_a\bigl(e^{V_a}(F_a-U_a)\bigr).
 \label{eq:separated-response}
\end{equation}
If $\dot P_a=s_aP_a$ with sufficient integrability, the last term is $\Cov_{Q_a}(F_a,s_a)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The product integral is differentiable because variation controls integration of bounded tests, and the test family is differentiable in the supremum norm. The difference quotient splits into the change in measure, source, and observable; cross remainders tend to zero. Thus
$Z_a'=\dot P_a(e^{V_a})+P_a(e^{V_a}\dot V_a)$ and
$(P_ae^{V_a}F_a)'=\dot P_a(e^{V_a}F_a)+P_a(e^{V_a}(\dot F_a+F_a\dot V_a))$.
Apply the quotient rule and collect $F_a-U_a$. When the derivative has density $s_a$, its contribution is $Q_a[s_a(F_a-U_a)]$, which is the displayed covariance. The score need not be centered under $Q_a$.
\end{proof}

For a finite report $R$ with positive cell probabilities, write
$J_a(y)=P_a(e^{V_a}\one_{R=y})$, $p_a(y)=P_a(R=y)$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \partial_a\log\frac{J_a(y)}{p_a(y)}
 =\frac{\dot P_a(e^{V_a}\one_y)+P_a(e^{V_a}\dot V_a\one_y)}{J_a(y)}
 -\frac{\dot P_a(\one_y)}{p_a(y)}.
 \label{eq:projected-response}
\end{equation}
This follows by differentiating two positive scalars. A moving report or moving path support needs a geometric correction rather than an unjustified common score.

\subsection{The complete first-order interface term}
Let a bounded domain be partitioned into finitely many moving smooth subdomains $D_\gamma(a)$. The density and tests are supported away from any fixed external boundary. Each internal interface $\Sigma$ has two sides, denoted $-$ and $+$, and a normal pointing from $-$ to $+$. Its parameter velocity in that direction is $v_\Sigma$. Put
\[
 J_a(F)=\sum_\gamma\int_{D_\gamma(a)}\rho_a(x)e^{V_{a,\gamma}(x)}F_{a,\gamma}(x)\dd x.
\]
All fields have $C^1$ branch extensions and a common integrable envelope; domains admit $C^1$ local transports.

\begin{theorem}[Shape response with both physical traces]\label{thm:shape}
Under these assumptions,
\begin{align}
 J_a'(F)
 &=\sum_\gamma\int_{D_\gamma(a)}e^{V_\gamma}
 [\dot\rho F_\gamma+\rho\dot F_\gamma+\rho F_\gamma\dot V_\gamma]\dd x\notag\\
 &\quad+\sum_\Sigma\int_\Sigma v_\Sigma\rho
 [e^{V_-}F_- -e^{V_+}F_+]\dd S.
 \label{eq:shape}
\end{align}
For $Z_a=J_a(1)>0$, the normalized derivative is
$[J_a'(F)-(J_a(F)/Z_a)J_a'(1)]/Z_a$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
On one subdomain pull the integral back through $\chi_a:D_\gamma(a_0)\to D_\gamma(a)$. With $w=\partial_a\chi_a\circ\chi_a^{-1}$, differentiation of its Jacobian yields
$\int_{D_\gamma(a)}[\partial_af+\nabla f\cdot w+f\operatorname{div}w]dx$.
The last two terms form $\operatorname{div}(fw)$. The divergence theorem gives the exterior-normal flux of that branch. On an internal interface the two exterior normals are opposite; pairing the \emph{same physical interface} produces $v_\Sigma(f_--f_+)$. Fixed outer boundaries contribute zero because of support; a genuinely moving outer boundary must be retained. Expanding $f=\rho e^VF$ gives \eqref{eq:shape}. The quotient formula is ordinary differentiation.
\end{proof}

At higher order use a common $C^{r+1}$ transport and differentiate on a fixed domain. The resulting finite Leibniz and Fa\`a di Bruno sums are specified in Appendix~\ref{app:jets}. Expanding them in physical coordinates can produce normal derivatives of delta currents and intersection terms. Products of unrelated distributions are not defined by merely writing such a sum; clean stratifications or a common transport have to justify it.

\subsection{Strong distributional differentiation}
\begin{lemma}[Dirac jets in a fixed negative Sobolev space]\label{lem:dirac}
On $\T^d$, if $s>d/2+r$, then $x\mapsto\delta_x$ is $C^r$ with values in $H^{-s}$ and all derivatives through order $r$ have bounded norm. If $X_a(z)$ and scalar weights $w_a(z)$ have bounded derivatives through order $r$, continuous in $a$ uniformly in $z$, and $\lambda$ is a finite measure, then
$\int w_a(z)\delta_{X_a(z)}\lambda(dz)$ is strongly $C^r$ in $H^{-s}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The Fourier coefficient of $\delta_x$ is $e^{-2\pi ik\cdot x}$. A derivative of order $j$ is bounded in squared $H^{-s}$ norm by a constant times
\[
 \sum_{k\in\Z^d}(1+|k|^2)^{-s}|k|^{2j},
\]
which converges for $s>d/2+r$. Modewise difference quotients converge and are dominated by this summable sequence, yielding norm derivatives and norm continuity. The finite chain rule and Leibniz rule give an integrable uniform majorant for the weighted composition. The Banach-valued fundamental theorem of calculus passes the derivatives through the Bochner integral. An embedding of a compact reporting interval into a circle, or a finite direct sum for discrete tags, gives the same conclusion for the reporting spaces used later.
\end{proof}

A derivative such as $\delta'$ is not generally a finite signed measure, let alone a score density relative to the original law. Negative-Sobolev differentiability therefore cannot replace the variation hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:regular-response}. The correct next step is pairing against a smooth source/test or applying a proved observation-smoothing map.

\begin{lemma}[Finite-order differentiable summation]\label{lem:sum}
Let $J_k(a)$ be $C^r$ in a common Banach space on a compact interval and suppose
$\sum_k\sup_a\norm{J_k^{(j)}(a)}<\infty$ for every $0\le j\le r$. Then $\sum_kJ_k$ is $C^r$ and its derivatives are the corresponding sums.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
All derivative series converge uniformly. For finite partial sums use
$S_M(a+h)-S_M(a)=\int_0^hS_M'(a+t)dt$. Pass to the uniform limit on both sides to obtain a strong first derivative. Repeat at each derivative level. No weak boundedness argument is used to assert norm differentiability.
\end{proof}
